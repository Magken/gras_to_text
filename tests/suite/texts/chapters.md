# The north kiln

## Night firing

The kiln at the north end of the yard was lit before the street lamps, and the first hour belonged to whoever had drawn the short straw. Helen had drawn it. She set the dampers by the card on the nail, then she set them again because the card had been written in a hurry and the hurry showed. The bricks took the heat without comment. A good kiln is a boring story for a long time, and then it is the only story.

She kept the book open on the stool even though the light was bad. The hour went in the first column. The colour of the spy-hole went in the second. The third stayed blank, and the blank was a decision, not a forgetfulness. Wind off the canal leaned on the shed and the shed leaned back. Somewhere a bicycle ticked past and did not stop.

At two in the morning the flame settled. It had been restless, climbing and dropping, and then it found a note and held it. She wrote the note as straw, which was close enough to be useful and not close enough to be a lie. Mr. Pell would have wanted a finer word. Mr. Pell was asleep, and the kiln did not require his vocabulary. She closed the spy-hole and listened to the tick of cooling metal that was not yet cooling.

## Morning count

Dawn came up the colour of dirty water. The racks in the yard were beaded with damp, and the upper pots looked softer than they were. She counted them anyway. Forty-two on the upper side, thirty-seven on the lower, and the two that had been missing on Monday were still missing. The crate of broken ware by the door had not gained them. A missing pot is a specific kind of annoyance, because it asks you to suspect a person and the yard had no person she wanted to suspect.

Dr. Hale arrived with the noon bell still a long way off, which meant he had not slept either, or he had slept and was pretending the street's title obliged him to witness things. He looked at the spy-hole. He nodded as if a nod were a measurement. Mrs. Adel came later, with the post in a damp packet and a complaint about the hill already prepared. The hill was not steep. The complaint was how she knocked.

They drank the strong tea from the mugs that did not match. The chipped one went to Mrs. Adel, by her own rule. Helen told them the flame had held. She did not tell them the word she had written. Some words survive a morning and some do not, and she wanted to see which kind straw was. The crane by the gate turned once in the wind and lifted nothing. Nobody remarked on it. The nothing had become local weather.

# The order book

## What the shop asked

The letter from the shop was plain, which Helen trusted more than she trusted praise. Six bowls of the pale clay, six of the dark, by the first of the month, price as last time. No speech about heritage. No request for a story to tie to each bowl. She read it twice and then she read it aloud to the shed, which was empty, because a plain order deserves to be heard at least once before it becomes work.

Mr. Pell heard the second reading, because he had come in for the ledger and the ledger lived on the rail. He was pleased by the numbers and displeased by the third column, which he could see from where he stood. A buyer, he said, does not pay for a blank. Helen said a buyer was not the shop, and the shop had asked for bowls, not for a diary of flames. The groove of the old argument was right there under their feet. They both stepped in it. Neither slipped.

She wrote the reply at the bench with clay drying on her wrists. The date, the numbers, the price. She did not write the blank into the letter, and she did not fill the blank in order to deserve the letter. The two pages had different jobs. Confusing them was how a yard started to lie in a tidy hand. She blotted the reply, addressed it, and set it under a tile so the door would not eat it.

## The margin

The real numbers lived in the margin of the firing book, where a buyer would not think to look and where Helen could find them in the dark. Monday's count, Tuesday's count, the two missing, the crate. She added the shop's twelve to a different line, because an order is not a census. Mixing them would make the missing pots look like a fulfilment problem, and they were not that. They were a hole in the room.

Mrs. Adel, reading over her shoulder, said the margin was a superstition. Helen said the columns were the superstition, if a superstition is a mark you keep after it has stopped telling the truth. They left it there. Friendship in the yard was mostly the decision not to finish certain sentences. The chipped mug was rinsed and turned upside down on the board. The chip clicked against the wood, a small flag.

By late afternoon the twelve bowls were chosen and set apart on a clean shelf. Six pale, six dark, none of them with a thumb mark, because the shop had not asked for thumb marks and Helen did not volunteer the private vice. She wrapped the feet of each bowl in paper. The paper came from a stack that had once been invoices. She liked that an invoice could end as packing. It was not a moral. It was a thrift.

# The canal side

## Gaps

She ate bread and a pear on the step and watched the canal go the colour of zinc. A rope dragged a long dark thread and the thread closed when the rope stopped. She began to count the gaps between the rings of that thread. She stopped at four. The gaps were the water's, and filing them would have muddied the book and the canal both. You can love a series without owning it. She was trying to learn the difference in public, which is the harder classroom.

A train passed on the far bank and the racks clicked. The click ran through the yard and ended at the kiln, which answered with a tick of its own, hotter and slower. Two rhythms, not one. Helen held them apart in her head the way she held the margin apart from the columns. Mr. Pell would have called that fussiness. The pots did not slump when she was fussy. She was willing to be complained about in exchange for that.

The buyer Mr. Pell feared did not come on Thursday after all. A note arrived instead, polite and short, moving the visit to the following week. Helen felt an ugly relief and did not dress it up. A postponed test is not a passed test. It is only time, and time in a yard is already full of clay that will dry whether you are ready or not. She pinned the note above the card of damper settings. The card was still in a hurry. The note was not. They made an odd pair.

## What was left

Toward evening she opened the kiln a finger's width. Heat came out dull and clean. The pots inside were shadows. They were seen, and then the door closed, and the seeing had been enough to know that nothing had stuck and nothing had fallen. Mr. Pell stood behind her and did not speak. She counted the silence as a kindness and did not audit it for fatigue. Some ledgers are a violence if you keep them on people.

She locked the gate after the others had gone. The crane was still. The racks were still. The twelve wrapped bowls waited on the clean shelf like a sentence she had already sent. The book was in the inside pocket of her coat. The third column was blank. The margin was not. On the walk home she passed the bus stop and let the buses be buses. The day had had enough numbers, and the street was allowed to keep its own.

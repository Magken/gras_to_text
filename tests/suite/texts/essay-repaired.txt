The kiln at the end of the yard cooled through the night, and the bricks ticked as they cooled. Dr. Hale met Mr. Pell and Mrs. Adel, and they stood still by the kiln. She wrote the firing times in a book. The first column was the hour. The second column was the colour of the flame. The third column stayed blank upon purpose.

The yard filled with the traffic of a working day. A boy carried shelves to the wall and dropped them too hard. Someone swore, and then someone laughed. The crane by the gate turned and stopped. It turned because the wind came off the canal.

She walked the length of the racks and said nothing. The pots on the upper rack were pale. The pots on the lower rack were darker, and thumbs had marked a few of them. She liked the marks, but she did not say so.

Mr. Pell hated the blank because a buyer was coming on Thursday. He said a blank looked like carelessness to a person who buys pots. She listened. She did not fill the column. He would sigh, and she would offer tea, and nothing in the book would change.

The tea was too strong and the mugs did not match. One mug had a chip at the rim. Mrs. Adel drank from it on purpose. Dr. Hale smiled at that, but he ignored the ledger.

She counted the pots on the racks. Forty-two sat on the upper rack and thirty-seven sat on the lower one. Two were missing. The broken ones sat in a crate by the clay bin. She wrote the numbers in the margin of the book.

A train passed on the far side of the canal, and the racks clicked. Nobody looked up. She thought of the buyer and of the lie she would not write. Both facts stayed in the book.

She ate bread and a pear at the bench. She watched the canal as it turned the colour of zinc. A rope dragged in the water, and a dark thread closed behind it. She counted the gaps in that thread, then stopped, because the gaps belonged to the water.

The afternoon was for repairs. Someone planed a shelf and sanded a lid. The hinge on the shed door moved again. The old holes stayed in the wood. The wood kept the record of every repair, and that was enough.

Mrs. Adel brought the post up the hill and complained about the climb. A shop wanted twelve bowls by Monday. The order was plain, and she was glad of it. It was the right kind of order.

She answered the letter at the bench. She wrote the date and the numbers. She wrote the price in the second column. She left the third column blank, as she always did.

Evening came, and Mr. Pell opened the kiln a crack. The heat came out in a slow breath. The pots inside were shadows. Nothing had slumped and nothing had stuck, and that was enough for the day. Mr. Pell stayed quiet.

She locked the gate at the end of the day. The crane was still. The third column in the book was still blank. She walked home by the canal and ignored the buses.

The kiln at the north end of the yard kept its heat through the night, and the bricks ticked as they cooled. Dr. Hale met Mr. Pell and Mrs. Adel before the noon bell, and they stood with their hands behind their backs as if the kiln might still be argued with. She had written the firing times in a book that smelled of ash. The first column was the hour. The second was the colour of the flame. The third was left blank upon purpose, because a blank line was the only honest mark for a thing she had not seen.

By midmorning the yard had filled with the ordinary traffic of a working day. A boy carried shelves from the shed to the wall and set them down too hard. Someone swore, then laughed, then swore again because the laugh had not fixed the cracked board. The crane by the gate turned once and stopped. It had nothing to lift. It turned because that was what it did when the wind came off the canal.

She walked the length of the drying racks and touched nothing. The pots on the upper rack were pale and dusty. The pots on the lower rack were darker, and a few of them still showed the mark of a thumb. She liked the thumb marks and she did not say so. Taste, in that yard, was a private vice. You could keep it if you did not announce it.

Mr. Pell wanted the third column filled. He said a blank looked like carelessness to a buyer, and a buyer was coming on Thursday, and Thursday was not a philosophical day. She listened. She did not fill the column. The argument was an old one and it had worn a groove, the way a latch wears a groove, and both of them knew which step came next. He would sigh. She would offer tea. Neither of them would change the book.

The tea was too strong and the mugs did not match. One had a chip at the rim that everyone avoided and that everyone also refused to throw away. Mrs. Adel drank from it on purpose. She said a chipped mug kept a person honest. Dr. Hale, who was not a doctor of anything the yard sold, smiled at that and did not smile at the ledger. His title was a habit the street had given him, and the street was often wrong, and he had stopped correcting it.

After tea she went back to the racks and counted. Forty-two pots on the upper side. Thirty-seven on the lower. Two were missing from the count she had made on Monday, and the missing ones were not broken, because the broken ones sat in a crate by the door with their labels still tied on. She wrote the numbers in the margin, not in the third column. The margin was hers. The columns were for the room.

A train passed on the far side of the canal and the racks trembled just enough to click. Nobody looked up. The click was part of the hour, like the bell and the crane and the smell of wet clay under the awning. She thought of the buyer and of the blank line, and she thought of how a person can be right about a fact and still be a poor guest. Mr. Pell was right that a buyer would ask. She was right that an invented colour was a lie. The yard had room for both truths and no procedure for them.

At noon she ate bread and a pear on the step, and she watched the water. The canal was the colour of zinc. A rope dragged in it and drew a long dark thread that closed as soon as the rope stopped. She counted the gaps between the rings of that thread, then stopped counting, because the gaps belonged to the water and not to the book. Borrowing them would have muddied both.

The afternoon was a series of small repairs. A shelf was planed. A lid was sanded. A hinge was moved from the left door to the right, and the old screw holes were left as they were, four dark points that said where the hinge had been. She liked evidence of that kind. It did not require a column. It did not require a buyer. It required only that someone later would wonder, and that the wondering would be answered by the wood itself.

Mrs. Adel brought the post and stayed long enough to complain about the hill. The hill was not steep. The complaining was a way of arriving. Inside the packet was a letter from a shop that wanted six bowls of the pale clay and six of the dark, and that wanted them by the first of the month, and that asked for nothing ornate. She was glad of the plainness. A plain order can be kept. A fancy one starts an argument before the clay is even wet.

She answered the letter at the bench, with the clay drying on her wrists. Dear shop, she wrote, and then she did not write Dear, because the shop was not a person she had met. She wrote the date and the numbers and the price. She wrote that the bowls would be ready, and she did not write that the third column of her book was blank. Some facts are for the yard. Some facts travel. The skill is knowing which is which, and she was still learning it, and she did not pretend otherwise.

Toward evening the kiln was opened a finger's width and then closed again. The heat that came out was dull and clean. The pots inside were shadows. They were seen for a moment and then they were not, and the moment was enough to know that nothing had slumped and nothing had stuck. Mr. Pell stood behind her and did not speak. The not speaking was a kindness, or it was fatigue, and she decided she did not need to know which.

She locked the gate after the others had gone. The crane was still. The racks were still. The book was in the inside pocket of her coat, and the third column was still blank, and the blank was still upon purpose. On the walk home she passed the bus stop and did not count the buses. The day had had enough numbers. The street could keep its own.

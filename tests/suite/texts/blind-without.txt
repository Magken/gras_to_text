The ovens at Mercer's came on at eleven, before the last of the pubs on Quay Street had emptied, and for the first hour the bakery smelled mostly of hot iron and old flour burning off the racks. Ada Mercer stood at the long table with her sleeves pushed past the elbow, listening to the building tick as it warmed. She had done this for thirty-one years.

The apprentice, Tomas, arrived at ten past, which was late, though she did not say so. He hung his coat on the hook beside the sacks and washed his hands for longer than he needed to, the way people do when they are waiting to be told something. Outside, a halyard rang against a mast in the marina, patient and without rhythm.

They began with the rye, because the rye needed the longest. Ada tipped the starter from its crock, and Tomas watched the grey mass slump into the bowl, sour and alive, breathing faintly against the steel. She had fed it every day since before he was born. He had asked her once whether it had a name, and she had looked at him as though the question were unkind.

By one o'clock the windows had fogged from the inside. The street beyond them was only a smear of sodium light and now and then the shape of a gull settling on the railings. Tomas shaped the loaves in silence. His hands were still too quick; he pressed the air out of the dough as if he were afraid of it. Ada came up behind him, laid her palm over his knuckles once, and took it away.

"Slower," she said. "It knows you're nervous." He laughed, then saw she was not joking, and slowed down. After that the dough seemed to give differently under his fingers, though he could not have said how. He thought of his father's hands on a steering wheel, the same unhurried pressure, the way the old man had driven as though the road might bruise.

At half past two there was a lull, with the loaves proving under linen and the first trays of rolls in the deck oven. Ada made tea in two chipped mugs and they drank it standing, leaning against the counter. Neither of them spoke. Somewhere down the harbour a trawler's engine turned over and caught, and the sound travelled up through the stones and into the soles of their feet.

Tomas wanted to ask her why she had never taken anyone on before him. The question had been sitting in his mouth for weeks. But the kitchen at this hour did not seem to invite questions. It seemed instead to hold things, the way the proving cloth held the warmth of the dough, keeping it from the air until it was ready.

The croissants came out at three, and the room changed. There was the butter first, and then something darker underneath it, almost caramel, almost smoke. Ada turned each tray by hand and looked at the colour for a long moment before she slid them onto the cooling racks. Tomas understood that she was not checking them so much as greeting them.

Rui arrived at four with the van, as he always did, reversing up onto the kerb to a noise of complaining springs. He came in stamping the cold off his boots and bringing the salt air with him, a folded newspaper under one arm that he would not read. He was younger than Ada and older than Tomas, and he treated them both with the same easy, faintly amused courtesy.

"Fog's coming in off the point," he said, taking a roll from the rack without asking. Ada did not stop him. She never had. He ate it in three bites, looking out at the street, and told them the café on Anchor Lane wanted an extra dozen of the seeded, and that the hotel had complained again that the rye was too sour, which it always was.

They loaded the van together, passing the trays hand to hand in a line that needed no discussion. The crates were warm against Tomas's chest. The fog had indeed arrived. It lay along the water like a second, paler harbour, and each lamp on the quay had taken on a soft halo, so that for a moment the whole street looked as if it were remembering itself.

When Rui had gone and his red tail-lights had dissolved at the corner, Ada stood for a while in the doorway with her arms folded. Tomas began to scrape down the table. The flour came up in long curls under the blade. He gathered it into his palm and did not know what to do with it, and in the end he let it fall into the bin like something he had been careful with.

"Tomorrow you'll feed the starter," Ada said, without turning around. He looked up. She was watching the first grey light find the masts one after another, as though she were counting them. He wanted to thank her, but understood that this, too, was something the room would not hold. So he nodded at her back and went on cleaning, and the ovens ticked as they began to cool.

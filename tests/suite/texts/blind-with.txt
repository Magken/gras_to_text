The bakery on Quay Street lit its ovens at ten, and the smell of warm iron drifted out under the door toward the moored boats. Marta Kell came in through the back with her keys held in a fist so they would not ring in the empty street. Her apprentice, Tobin, was already sitting on the flour sacks with his cap pulled low over a face that had not yet decided to wake up. She told him to wash his hands. He did. The dough from the afternoon had risen under its cloths, and it lay along the long table in pale humps that looked like sleeping animals. Marta lifted one cloth and pressed a finger into the dough.

The dent filled slowly. That was the sign she wanted. Tobin watched her face and not the dough, because the face told him more. She kept a ledger for the bread that hung on a nail by the ovens. Each loaf had a line. The first mark was the weight. The second was the time.

The oven had a temper. It ran hot at the back and cool by the door, so the loaves had to be turned at the halfway point. Tobin forgot this on his third night. Marta said nothing about it then. She set the burnt loaves upon the sill for the gulls in the morning. He understood the lesson better than any word.

By midnight the harbour had gone quiet, and the only sound from outside was the slap of water against the stone steps below the fish market. Marta shaped the rye while Tobin cut the tops of the white loaves with a razor that she kept in a tin of its own. The cuts had to be quick and shallow, as if the blade were only asking the dough a question. His were too deep. The bread split along them in the heat and opened like a mouth. She held one up to the lamp and turned it slowly. He waited for her to speak.

Sully arrived at two with the van, which had once been white and was now the colour of the road it had driven for eleven years. He was a big man who moved carefully around the trays, as though the bread might bruise if he breathed on it. He brought news from the other end of the coast in the same slow voice every night. A trawler had come in late. The lighthouse keeper had a new dog. The price of butter was up again at the co-op, and he said this last one to Marta directly, because he knew she would care. She did care.

The three of them drank tea standing up. Nobody sat in the bakery after one o'clock. The stools were for the afternoon girls who iced the buns. Sully held his cup in both hands and blew across it. Tobin asked him about the dog. The dog was called Admiral. One ear stood up and one did not.

Marta went back to the ovens before the tea was gone. She opened the iron door and the heat came out at her like a crowd. The first rye was ready. She knocked on the bottom of each loaf and listened. A good loaf sounded hollow. A bad one sounded like a closed door.

The racks filled by three. Tobin carried them from the ovens to the front of the shop, and he set them down too fast. Marta told him to slow his feet. He slowed them for a while. Then he forgot again, and a tray of rolls slid against the rail. He looked at her. Nothing fell.

Sully loaded the van in a fixed order. The hotel came first, then the café by the ferry, then the old people's home on the hill. He knew which door stuck and which cook would be awake. The crates went in at the back with the hotel crate nearest the doors. He counted them twice. Marta counted them a third time from the step. Neither of them said why.

The van pulled away at half past three. Its lights swung across the wet cobbles and the shuttered windows of the chandlery. Tobin stood in the doorway until the sound was gone. The street was blue now and very still. A cat walked the top of the harbour wall. It did not look at him.

Inside, Marta was writing in the ledger with a stub of pencil that she sharpened on the edge of the bread knife, and her letters were small and leaned hard to the right. She wrote the weights and the times, and in the last column she wrote a word for each batch that only she could read. Tobin had tried to read it once over her shoulder while she was busy at the sink. He had got nothing from it but a few letters and a sense that he had been rude. The column was hers. She closed the book.

The sky over the breakwater began to grey. The first fishermen came up the steps with their hands in their pockets. They bought the broken loaves from the side window for half the price. Marta saved the broken ones for them on purpose. Tobin had thought it was an accident at first. It was not.

At six the afternoon girls were still asleep somewhere up the hill, and the shop was full of bread. Marta hung her apron on the nail beside the ledger. Tobin swept the flour from the floor into a grey drift by the door. She watched him do it and said it was good. He did not know whether she meant the floor or the bread or the night. He did not ask. The ovens ticked behind them in the quiet.

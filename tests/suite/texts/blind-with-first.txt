The bakery on Quay Street kept its ovens lit from ten at night until the gulls began, and the windows fogged from the inside as the first trays went in. Mrs. Orme came down the back stair at a quarter past ten with her sleeves already rolled and the keys in her fist. The apprentice was waiting in the yard because she had told him not to knock. His name was Tam. He was sixteen and he had flour on his boots from the night before, which she noticed and did not mention. The door stuck. She put her shoulder to it.

The dough had risen in the cold room since the afternoon. It sat in four tubs under damp cloths. Tam lifted a cloth and looked, as if the dough might have gone somewhere. Mrs. Orme told him to wash his hands first. He washed them at the deep sink. The water from the tap was brown for a moment and then clear. She weighed the first piece by hand and did not use the scale.

The scale was for Tam. She had bought it the week he started, because a boy needed a number before he could trust his palms. He used it for every loaf. She watched the needle over his shoulder and said nothing when it settled. The loaves came out even. They were not yet good. That was a different thing, and it took longer.

By midnight the harbour had gone quiet except for the halyards knocking against the masts, a sound that came in under the door with the smell of salt and diesel. Mrs. Orme shaped the rye in the old way, folding each piece toward her with the heel of her hand until the skin drew tight across the top. Tam tried to copy her. His loaves slumped. She took one from him and turned it over and showed him the seam. It was open like a mouth. He closed it and tried again, and this time the skin held, though the loaf that came out of his hands was lopsided and he knew it.

The ovens were older than the building that held them, and Mrs. Orme spoke of them as though they had come with the lease and would stay after it ran out. The left one ran hot at the back. The right one had a door that did not quite meet its frame, so the heat leaked out along the hinge in a thin shimmer that Tam could see if he stood at an angle. She kept a book on the shelf above the flour bins. In it she wrote the times and the heat for every bake. There was a column for the weather. There was a column she left empty.

Tam asked her once what the empty column was for. She said it was for the night something went wrong that she could not explain. He looked at the page. The column was blank all the way down. She said that was the point. He did not follow, but he kept quiet.

At two the trays came out. The crust on the first loaves cracked as they cooled, a small sound like gravel under a boot. Tam stood by the racks and listened. Mrs. Orme did not listen. She was already cutting butter into the flour for the morning rolls. Her knife moved quickly and never touched the board. The kitchen was warm now.

Mr. Keel came at half past three in the van with the dented side. He was a heavy man who drove for three bakeries on the coast road and talked about all of them. He stood in the doorway with his cap in his hand. Mrs. Orme gave him tea in the chipped mug. He drank it standing. He said the bakery at Merrow had burned its batch again. She said nothing to that.

The crates were stacked by the door in the order of the route. The café on the corner came first. The hotel came last because its kitchen did not open until six. Tam carried the crates out to the van and set them down too hard. Mr. Keel told him to mind the rolls. The boy set the next one down gently, as if it might wake.

Mr. Keel had a map of the route taped to the dashboard. It was soft with handling and the names were half gone. He did not need it. He kept it there because his wife had drawn it for him in the first year. Tam saw the pencil marks when he leaned in with the last crate. He did not ask about them.

When the van had gone Mrs. Orme sat down upon the flour sack by the back door, which was the only time in the night that she sat, and she let her hands rest open on her knees. Tam swept the floor around her because she did not ask him to move. The broom caught the loose flour and pushed it in soft drifts toward the drain. Out on the water a fishing boat was coming in with its lights still lit against the grey. She watched it until it passed behind the harbour wall and the gulls rose from the roofs to meet it. Then she stood.

She took down the book and opened it on the bench. She wrote the times and the heat and the weather. Tam watched her pen come to the last column. It hovered there. She had seen his lopsided loaf go into the crate for the hotel. She did not write anything. The column stayed blank.

The light came up grey over the harbour. Tam untied his apron and hung it on the nail by the sink. Mrs. Orme told him to come at ten the next night and not to knock. He said he would not. The book went back upon the shelf with its empty column still empty, and that was upon purpose. The ovens ticked as they cooled.

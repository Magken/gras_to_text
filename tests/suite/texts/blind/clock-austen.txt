Mrs. Harriet Pell had mended clocks for thirty years, and she did not believe that any clock was ever beyond saving, though she allowed that some parishes were.

"You will hold the lamp," she told her nephew, "and you will not touch the escapement unless I ask you to."

Thomas, who was nineteen and had come to her only in the spring, said that he understood perfectly. He did not.

The tower of St. Aldric's was narrow and smelled of pigeons and old rope, and the clock sat upon its oak frame like a patient who has been neglected too long by relations who ought to have known better. Its hands had stopped at twenty past four on some afternoon nobody could remember.

"The vicar says it must strike on Saturday," Thomas said. "He says the bride's mother will not hear of a wedding without bells and the hour together."

"Then the bride's mother shall have them, if the wheels allow it."

She knelt, lifted the dust from the great wheel with a soft brush, and looked at it for a long while without speaking. Thomas held the lamp higher.

"Is it very bad?"

"It is very dirty, which is not the same thing, though people so often confuse the two that I have given up correcting them."

On Monday they took the movement apart. Harriet laid each piece upon a linen cloth in the order she removed it; she named them aloud as she went, and consequently Thomas learned more in that one morning than he had in all the weeks before. He learned that the pallets were worn, that the pivots were dry, and that his aunt hummed when she was pleased.

"You are humming," he said.

"I am not."

She was, and she went on doing it until noon.

On Tuesday the vicar climbed the ladder to see their progress, and he stood in the trapdoor with his hat in his hand, breathing hard and looking at the scattered brass with an expression of real alarm.

"Mrs. Pell, I had understood that you meant to repair it, not to distribute it about the floor."

"I cannot repair what I cannot see, Mr. Gould. You would not ask a surgeon to mend a leg through the trousers."

He went down again rather quickly. Thomas laughed until she told him to stop, and then he laughed a little more.

"You should not have said that about the trousers," he said.

"Perhaps not. But he will leave us alone now, which is what I wanted, and therefore I shall not apologise."

On Wednesday it rained. The water came through a gap in the louvres and ran along the beam above them, and Harriet moved the linen cloth twice before she was satisfied that nothing would be spoiled.

"Tell me about the girl," she said, while she cleaned the pivots with pith.

"Which girl?"

"The one who is to be married. You know her; you have gone red."

Thomas said that he knew her only a little, that her name was Ellen Marsh, and that she had once lent him a book which he had never returned. His aunt did not look up from her work.

"And the man she is to marry?"

"He is a farmer from over the hill, and I believe he is a very respectable person."

"That is what people say about a man when they can find nothing else to say about him."

Thomas did not answer. The rain went on.

On Thursday they began to put the clock back together, and it was slower work than taking it apart, because every wheel had to find its place and every pin had to be driven home without bending. Harriet did the fine work herself; she let Thomas fit the larger arbors, and she watched him do it with her arms folded.

"Gently. You are not shoeing a horse."

"I am being gentle."

"You are being gentle in the manner of someone who is thinking about something else."

He set the arbor down. For a moment neither of them spoke, and the only sound in the tower was the drip from the louvres.

"I ought to have given the book back," he said at last. "I kept it because it was hers, and now it is too late to give it back without looking foolish."

"It is never too late to return a thing to its owner. It is only too late to pretend you had no reason for keeping it."

On Friday evening she wound the weights up for the first time, and they stood together beside the frame while the pendulum began its long swing. The escapement caught. The clock ticked, heavily and evenly, as if it had merely been asleep.

"Will it strike?" Thomas asked.

"We shall find out at seven."

At seven the hammer lifted and fell, and the bell sounded through the wet evening over the roofs of the village; down in the lane a dog barked at it, and somebody in a cottage opened a window to listen.

Harriet put her hand flat upon the oak frame. She was humming again, and this time she did not deny it.

On Saturday morning the bells rang and the clock struck eleven exactly as the bride came up the path. Thomas stood at the back of the church with the book in his coat, and after the service he gave it to her.

"You kept it a long time," Ellen said, smiling.

"I did. I am sorry for that."

She told him it did not matter in the least, and he believed her, because the clock above them had just begun to strike the quarter.

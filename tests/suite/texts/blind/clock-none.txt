The clock in St. Aldhelm's tower had stopped at nineteen minutes past four. Nobody in the village could say which afternoon it had happened, only that for most of the spring the hands had pointed the same way. The farmers stopped looking up at it, and the children learned to tell the time from the bus.

Maud Pellow came up the lane on the Monday with her tools rolled in oilcloth under one arm and her nephew behind her carrying the rest. Tobias was sixteen and tall in a way he hadn't finished getting used to. He knocked the toolbox against every gatepost they passed. The vicar met them at the lychgate with a ring of keys and a worried look. There was a wedding on Saturday, he said, the Hollis girl and the boy from the garage, and they had asked for the bells. The bells ran off the clock. Could it be done?

Maud looked up at the tower as if it had asked her the question itself. "We'll see what it wants," she said.

The stair was a spiral of worn stone so narrow that Tobias had to climb it sideways. At the top, behind a door swollen into its frame, the mechanism sat in its iron cage like a sleeping animal. It was a flatbed movement, the going train on one side and the striking train on the other, and the pendulum rod ran down through a slot in the floor into darkness. Dust covered everything, and pigeons had been in. Maud walked all the way round it once without touching anything, then again with her hand held just above the metal, the way you'd feel for heat.

"Well," she said. "It's not dead. It's just tired."

They spent the first day cleaning. Tobias had expected to do something cleverer than that, and he said so around the middle of the afternoon, with paraffin running down his wrists. Maud didn't look up from the escapement. Most of mending, she told him, was taking away whatever had been added. Time adds things: grit, damp, old oil gone to varnish, the bodges of whoever came before. "Somebody in about nineteen-fifty put a washer here that has no business being here," she said, and held it up to the slatted light. "Probably a nice man. Probably in a hurry."

On Tuesday it rained, and the tower filled with the sound of water running in the gutters. They took the going train down wheel by wheel and laid the parts out on a sheet in order, like a sentence spelled out in brass. Tobias worked out that if he said nothing, Maud would sometimes talk. She told him about his grandfather, who had kept a watch shop in Truro and was never on time for anything. She told him about the year she spent in Switzerland and hated, and about the first clock she ever stopped on purpose, in a house where a woman had died, because the family asked her to. "People think a clock is about moving forward," she said. "Half the time they want it for the opposite reason. They want to be told where they were."

He asked whether she'd ever been married. She put down her loupe.

"Nearly," she said. "Once." Then she handed him a pivot to polish, and he understood that was all he was getting, and he found he didn't mind.

By Wednesday the wheels were back and the pendulum was hung, but it wouldn't hold its beat. It ticked, hesitated, ticked, like somebody who can't make up their mind to go into a room. Maud lay on her back on the boards with her head under the frame for most of an hour. Tobias held the torch and watched a spider rebuild in the corner what he'd knocked down the day before. When at last she slid out, her hair was grey with dust, and she laughed, which surprised him. The floor had settled. Across two hundred years the whole tower had leaned a few degrees towards the churchyard, and the clock had been trying to keep time on a slope all along.

They shimmed the cage level with slips of oak. Tobias cut them in the porch with a borrowed saw while the Hollis girl's mother went in and out with measuring tapes and ribbon. She asked what he was making. "Level," he said, and she looked at him as though he were being rude, which he hadn't meant.

On Thursday the clock went, and it kept going. Maud set it against the radio pips and then leaned on the rail and simply listened, her eyes half shut, for a long time. Tobias listened too. From inside, the sound wasn't like a clock at all. It was heavier and slower, closer to breathing, and the tower seemed to fill with it.

The striking train took all of Friday. The hammer had to be adjusted to within a hair, and the count wheel had a broken tooth that Maud rebuilt with a file and a scrap of old brass, working by the one window until the light went. At nine that evening she let Tobias release the fly himself. The hammer lifted, hung, and fell. The note that came out of the bell above them went through the stone and into their feet, and out over the dark roofs of the village. Down in the lane a dog began to bark, and someone opened a door to see what had happened.

On Saturday they sat at the back of the church in clean shirts, because the vicar had insisted. The bride came in late, as brides do. When the vows were over and the bells began, Tobias looked at his aunt. She wasn't watching the couple. Her face was turned up towards the tower, and she was counting under her breath, the way she might count a pulse.

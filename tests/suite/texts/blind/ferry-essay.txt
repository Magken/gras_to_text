The ferry at the bend of the river ran until the ice came, and the two sisters who worked it kept a lamp on the landing through the short afternoons. Ruth took the pole and Agnes took the rope. The raft was eleven planks wide and had been tarred in October by the two of them, working from the far end toward the shore. The river was black under the lamp and moved faster than it looked from the bank, which was a thing that visitors never believed until they stood upon it. The fare was two coins or a sack of something useful. Agnes kept the coins in a tin that had once held tea. The tin sat on a shelf in the hut, beside the lamp oil and the ledger, and nobody but the sisters knew how light it had become.

The last crossing was at four. After four the rope was slackened and the pole was hung on its pegs. Ruth had made that rule in the first winter. She did not explain it to anyone. The rule held because the dark on the water was a different dark from the dark on land. Agnes had never argued with it, though she had wanted to more than once.

On the night of the stranger the snow had stopped. The yard was blue and still. Agnes was scraping the pot when the bell on the far post rang twice. The bell was an old cow bell hung from a nail. It rang only when someone pulled the cord on the other bank. Ruth looked up from the ledger and did not move. The bell rang again, slower this time, as if the hand on the cord had grown patient.

Agnes went out first with the lamp held low, and Ruth followed her to the edge of the landing where the planks were furred with frost. A man stood on the far bank with a bag at his feet and his hat in his hand. The lamp could not reach him. They saw him only as a shape against the pale field, a shape that did not shift its weight or stamp against the cold the way a man who had walked far would stamp. Agnes called across that the ferry was closed until morning. The man answered that he knew, and that he would pay what the morning cost and a little more for the dark. His voice carried well over the water, which Ruth noted and did not like.

The sisters stood a while and said nothing to each other. The quarrel was old and it had a shape, the way a path across a field has a shape, and both of them knew where it turned. Agnes wanted the coins. Ruth wanted the rule. Agnes said the tin was nearly empty and the ice would come in a week and then there would be no coins at all until March. Ruth said the river did not care about March. The silence after that was a surrender, or it was only the cold, and Agnes let it go without asking which.

They took the raft out together. Ruth set the pole and Agnes drew the rope hand over hand. The water talked under the planks. Halfway across, the lamp showed the man's boots and then his coat and then his face. It was a young face and very tired. He stepped aboard without being asked and set the bag down in the middle, where the weight would sit best, as if he had done this on other rivers.

Nobody spoke on the way back. Agnes watched the rope. Ruth watched the man. He kept his eyes on the lamp and his hands in his lap. The bag was leather and wet at the seams. Something inside the bag moved once, a small turn, and then lay still. Ruth shifted the pole to her other hand so that the free one was nearer the knife on her belt.

At the landing the man paid in silver. Agnes held the coins under the lamp and counted them twice. There were more than the fare and more than the dark. She looked up to say so, but the man had already lifted his bag and begun to walk toward the road. The road led to the town, and the town was six miles off, and six miles was a long way to carry a thing that moved.

Ruth called after him. He stopped. She asked what was in the bag, and her voice was harder than she meant it to be. The man was quiet for a time. Then he knelt and opened the bag in the snow. A dog lay inside, curled on a folded blanket. The dog was old and grey about the mouth and did not lift its head.

He said the dog had been his father's. His father had died on the other side on Tuesday. The house there was sold and the new people did not want an old dog. He had carried it to the river because the dog could no longer walk far. He had not wanted to wait for morning in a field. That was all.

Agnes put the coins back into his hand, all but the two that made the fare, and he took them without a word because he was too tired to argue. Ruth went into the hut and came out with the blanket from the bench, which was thicker than the one in the bag and smelled of tar and smoke. She laid it over the dog and tucked the ends under so the wind would not find them. The man thanked her twice. Ruth told him the Carrow farm was a mile up the road and that the woman there would give him a barn for the night if he said the ferry had sent him. He nodded and closed the bag with great care.

They watched him go until the dark took him. The lamp hissed. Agnes said the tin was lighter now than it would have been. Ruth said it was. Neither sister moved to go in. The river went on past them, black and quick, and the ice was a week away or less.

Later Ruth opened the ledger and wrote the crossing under the date. In the first column she wrote the hour. In the second she wrote the fare. The third column was for the name, and she left it empty upon purpose. An empty line was the one true mark for a man who had not given one. Agnes read it over her shoulder and said nothing, and then she turned down the lamp.

The river froze from the edges inward that year, and by the second week of December the ferry had only a channel the width of three boats to work in. Every morning Ilse went out with the long iron bar and broke the new skin of ice along the landing. Her sister Maren watched from the wheelhouse and counted the strokes under her breath, the way their father had, as if the number meant something.

They had run the crossing together for eleven winters. In summer there were other boats: pleasure launches, the hotel's white skiff, boys on rafts made of doors. In winter there was only theirs, a flat-bottomed scow with a diesel engine older than both of them, and a cable strung bank to bank so the current could not take her. Farmers used it, and the doctor, and now and then a hearse. The sisters charged by the axle and did not charge for grief.

Ilse was the elder and the one who talked. She knew which of the Hallorans was drinking again and whose barn had burned, and she said so to anyone standing on the deck long enough to listen. Maren kept the engine and the ledger. She had a way of going still while other people spoke that made them say more than they meant to, and then feel they had been forgiven for it.

By the rules posted on the shed door, the last crossing was at four. At that latitude in December, four o'clock was already dusk. The light went blue first, then grey, and then it was simply gone, and the far bank became a guess.

The stranger came at twenty past six.

Maren heard him before she saw him. There was a knocking on the shed, not loud, spaced out, like someone who expected to be refused and wanted to be refused politely. She lit the storm lantern and went out. Ilse was already up from the stove with her coat half on.

He was a tall man in a town overcoat, with no hat, and snow had settled in his hair and stayed there without melting, which Maren noticed and put away for later. He had no bag. He said he needed to cross tonight. He said it the way people say the name of an illness.

"We're closed," Ilse said. "There's a room at Fenner's. He'll take you over at first light himself if you ask him, he's got nothing else to do."

"I know Fenner's," the man said. "I can't wait for first light."

Ilse would have argued. That was how she was kind, by arguing until the other person gave in or proved they had a reason. But Maren was looking at the man's shoes. They were thin leather, soaked black to the laces, and they were not the shoes of anyone who had walked from Fenner's. They were the shoes of someone who had walked a long way over ground that didn't want him.

"Four dollars," Maren said. "Cash."

Ilse turned to her, and Maren gave her the small shake of the head they had used since they were girls, which meant later.

They took the scow out by lantern. The engine coughed and caught. Ilse stood at the bow with the bar, feeling for ice she couldn't see, and the cable sang overhead as it took the weight. The man stood in the middle of the deck with his hands in his pockets. He did not hold the rail. Maren watched him in the wheelhouse glass, where her own face sat over his like a second, paler passenger.

Halfway across, the channel had narrowed. Ilse struck and struck, and the ice gave with a sound like a plate breaking in another room. The man said, to no one, "It was open when I was a boy. All winter. You could hear it running under the house."

"Which house?" Ilse said without turning.

He named it. It was the Aldous place on the far bank. It had been empty since before either sister was born, and it had burned in the flood year, and all that was left was a chimney standing in the birches like a finger held up for quiet.

Ilse stopped working the bar. The scow drifted a little on the cable and swung.

"Keep on," Maren called. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We'll lose the channel."

So Ilse kept on. Nobody said anything else. The far bank came up out of the dark a piece at a time: first the pilings, then the landing, then the birches silver in the lantern, and behind them the black shape of the chimney.

The man stepped off before they had tied up. He turned, took four dollars out of his coat, and put them on the capstan, the old soft bills their father used to call horse blankets. Then he went up the bank, and the dark closed behind him without any sound of footsteps.

On the way back neither of them spoke until they had passed the middle. Then Ilse said, "You knew."

"I knew he'd walk out on the ice if we didn't take him."

"And that was worse?"

Maren eased the throttle. The home landing appeared, their own lamp in the shed window, the one they always left burning. "It was colder," she said.

In the morning the channel had closed completely, bank to bank, and it stayed closed until March. The four dollars were still in the ledger when they sold the ferry, pressed flat between October and November. Neither sister would spend them, and neither ever asked the other why.

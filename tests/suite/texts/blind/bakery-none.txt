The ovens were lit at eleven, the way they had been lit every night for thirty-one years, and Harrow stood in front of them with his hand flat against the steel door as if he were taking the pulse of an animal. Outside, Quay Street had gone quiet. The only sound was the halyards knocking against the masts in the marina, like someone patiently trying doors.

Nell arrived at quarter past, which was late, and said nothing about it, which was worse. She hung her coat on the hook beside the flour sacks and tied her apron twice around her waist, because it had been made for a larger person. It was probably Harrow's wife's, though nobody had said so. She was nineteen and had been with him since March. She could shape a batch of rolls now without looking at her hands. He had not told her this was unusual, because telling her would have changed it.

"The rye's been proving since nine," he said. "Knock it back and weigh it into sixes."

She did. The dough sighed under her fists. Rye has a smell that white flour never has, something sour and cellar-like, and it rose around her and settled into her hair, where it would stay until she washed it in the afternoon.

For the first two hours they spoke only in weights and times. Four hundred. Eight-fifty. Twenty minutes at two-thirty, then drop it. The radio on the shelf above the sink played the shipping forecast, which neither of them listened to and both of them would have missed. Viking, North Utsire, South Utsire. Harrow once said it was the only poem he knew by heart. Nell had laughed, and then realised he meant it.

At one o'clock he let her do the croissants alone.

He did not announce it. He simply went into the back to count the trays of eggs and stayed there longer than counting eggs required. Nell stood at the marble with the block of laminated dough in front of her, cold from the fridge, its layers invisible and entirely present, like the rings in a tree. She rolled it out. She cut the triangles with the long wheel, nicking each base with the tip of the knife the way he did. Then she rolled them from the wide end toward the point, not too tight, leaving room for the thing she could not see to happen in the oven. When she looked up, he was standing in the doorway with an egg in each hand, as if he had forgotten what he had gone in for.

"Too tight," he said. Then, after a moment: "That one. Not the others."

Dev came at half past three, as he always did, reversing the van up the slope from the harbour with its hazards ticking orange against the shop window. He came in through the back with the cold still on him, rubbing his hands, a big man who moved carefully in small rooms. He drove for four bakeries along the coast and claimed Harrow's was the only one where anyone offered him coffee. That was not true, but it was the only one where he accepted it.

"There's a trawler in," he said. "Belgian. Whole quay smells of diesel and cod."

"They'll want bread," Harrow said.

"They'll want the café open, is what they'll want."

Nell poured him coffee from the flask. He held it in both hands, leaned against the doorframe, and watched her brush the croissants with egg. She felt the watching and did not mind it. Dev had a daughter her age in Glasgow whom he did not see. He had told her this once, in the same tone he used for traffic on the coast road, and she had understood that the flatness was not indifference but a kind of load-bearing wall.

"Those look proper," he said.

"They're not baked yet."

"I can tell anyway."

The first batch came out at four. Harrow slid the peel under the tray and drew it from the oven in one long movement. The heat rolled out into the room and fogged the window, and for a moment the three of them stood without speaking, looking at the croissants the way people look at a view. They had risen and opened along their seams into brittle amber coils. The tight one had split sideways and leaned against its neighbour like a drunk.

Harrow picked up the split one and broke it in half. Steam came out of it. He studied the honeycomb of air inside for a long time, then gave one half to Nell and one half to Dev and took none for himself.

"Eat that," he said. "That's the one we don't sell."

They loaded the van together: crates of rye and white and seeded, the croissants in paper-lined boxes, and two trays of the cinnamon knots that the hotel on the Esplanade ordered and its guests left half-eaten on their plates. Dev stacked them by a system only he understood and closed the doors with his hip.

The sky over the harbour had not yet begun to lighten, but it had begun to think about it. The trawler sat low at the quay with its deck lamps still burning. A man in yellow oilskins was hosing down the boards, and the water ran off the edge in a sheet that caught the light. The gulls had started. They always started before anything else.

Dev raised a hand and drove off down the slope. Nell stood in the doorway with her arms folded against the cold and watched the tail-lights turn at the bottom of Quay Street and go.

Behind her, Harrow was scraping down the marble: the long stroke of the blade, the pause, the tap against the bin. In an hour the shop would open, and the first customers would come in wanting the loaf from the left, not that one. They would not know that anything had happened in the night, because nothing had, except that the bread was there.

She went back in, picked up the other scraper, and started on the far end of the bench. They worked toward each other without speaking until they met in the middle.

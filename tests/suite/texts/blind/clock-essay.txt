The church clock at Hollin had stopped at twenty past four on the Monday, and the whole village had noticed it by the time the milk came round. Maud Carrow climbed the tower stair with her tool roll under one arm and her nephew Tom behind her carrying the lamp. The wedding was on Saturday. The bride's mother had already been to the forge and the post office and the vicarage to say that a stopped clock was a poor sign for a marriage. Maud did not hold with signs. She held with brass and oil and the weight of a pendulum, which could be measured and set right if a person had the patience for it. The stair turned eleven times before the door.

The room behind the door was small and cold. Pigeons had been in through the louvres. The movement stood in its iron frame like a loom with the threads cut. Tom set the lamp on the ledge and waited. Maud walked round the frame once and did not touch it. Then she knelt by the going train and looked at the teeth of the great wheel for a long time.

"It is the pallets," she said at last. The boy asked what pallets were. She showed him the two small hooks on the anchor that caught the escape wheel and let it go, one tooth at a time. One hook was worn to a slope. The wheel had slipped past it and run until the weight struck the floor of the case. That was all. A clock can fail from a single tooth.

They took the anchor out on Tuesday, and Maud wrapped it in a cloth and carried it down to her shop on Mill Lane as if it were a bird that might still wake. Tom had never been let into the back room before, where the lathe stood under a sheet and the files hung in the order of their fineness along the wall. She put him to cleaning the pivots with pegwood while she heated the steel. The boy worked slowly at first. By the afternoon he had found the rhythm of it and hummed to himself without knowing that he did. Maud heard the humming and let it go on, because a boy who hums is a boy who is not bored.

The bride's mother came to the shop on Wednesday and stood in the doorway with her gloves on, asking whether the clock would be going by Saturday or whether she ought to hire a man from the town. Maud said the clock would be going. The woman asked how Maud could be sure of it. Maud said she was not sure of the clock, but she was sure of the work, and the work was nearly done, and done work was the only kind she promised. Mrs. Leach looked at the lathe and the files and the boy with his pegwood. She went away without saying good day.

The silence was rudeness, or it was worry, and Maud found she did not mind which. Tom asked if they would really finish. She said they would finish on Thursday and test on Friday. The testing was the part that mattered. Anyone could put a clock together. Keeping a clock true for a week was another matter.

On Thursday they carried the anchor back up the eleven turns of the stair. The boy held the lamp steady while Maud fitted the arbor into its bushes. She asked him for the small screwdriver and he gave her the large one. She handed it back without a word. He found the right one the second time. The anchor rocked once under her finger and the escape wheel moved a single tooth.

Maud hung the weights and set the pendulum swinging by hand. The beat was uneven. The tick and the tock came too close together and then too far apart, like a man limping. She loosened the crutch and moved it a hair to the left. The beat grew even. Tom counted it against his own pulse and said it was faster than his heart. She said that was right, since the clock had less to worry about.

They set the hands by the station clock, which Tom had gone down to read. He came back up with his face red from the stair. The minute hand on the east face stuck at the quarter. Maud climbed out onto the leads behind the dial with the boy holding her belt. A nest had been built in the motion work. She took it out twig by twig and put it in her pocket.

The pocket was full of straw when they came down. The vicar met them in the porch. He thanked them, and he asked whether the bell would strike for the wedding. Maud said the striking train was sound. She had looked at it on Monday. The vicar said that was a mercy upon them all.

On Friday Maud went up alone at dawn to see what the night had done, and she found the clock four seconds slow, which was less than she had feared and more than she would allow. She lifted the rating nut a half turn and stood with her watch in her hand until the church was light. Tom arrived at seven with bread and cheese wrapped in a cloth from his mother. He sat on the step by the door and ate his share while she watched the pendulum. The boy asked whether she ever got tired of waiting on a clock. She told him the waiting was most of the trade, and the rest of the trade was knowing when to stop waiting.

By the evening the clock had held. Maud wrote the time and the error in the little book she kept for Hollin. The first entry in it was in her father's hand, from before she was born. Tom read the entries over her shoulder. He asked why some lines had nothing after the date. She said those were the weeks her father had not climbed the stair, and he would not write down a thing he had not seen.

On Saturday the bell struck eleven as the bride came up the path. Mrs. Leach looked at the tower and then looked away. Maud stood at the back of the crowd with the boy beside her. Tom counted the strokes under his breath. There were eleven. He grinned at her, and she let him see that she was pleased.

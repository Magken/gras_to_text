The oven at the back of the bakery on Quay Street held its heat from the evening before, and the tiles above it were warm to the hand. Marta came in at eleven with the keys on a string and the apron already tied. The apprentice was waiting under the awning with his collar up against the wind off the water. His name was Tobin and he was seventeen. Marta let him in without a word. The dough had risen in the cold room while the street slept, and the tubs sat in a row with their lids pushed up like hats that did not fit. She lifted the first lid and pressed a thumb into the skin.

The dough came back slowly. That was good. Tobin hung his coat on the nail by the flour sacks. The sacks were stacked to the height of a man. Marta weighed out the first batch on the brass scale and wrote the number on the board. The board was for the night. The day girls had their own.

Tobin shaped the loaves the way she had shown him in the spring. His hands were still too quick. The rounds came out tight on one side and slack on the other. Marta took one from the bench and turned it in her palms until the seam closed. She set it back among the others. Tobin watched and said nothing. The next one was better.

At half past one the harbour bell rang for the tide, and the sound came up the street and in under the door as if it had business upon the premises. The boats would be going out soon with their lamps lit and the men on deck in their oilskins. Marta did not look up from the peel. She had heard that bell every night for twenty-two years, and she knew the hour by it better than by the clock above the sink. The clock was eight minutes slow. Nobody had fixed it. The first trays went into the oven, and the heat pushed out against their faces when she opened the door.

The quarrel between them was about salt, and it came round most nights at about this hour, the way the tide came round, so that both of them knew the lines before either spoke. Tobin thought the rye wanted more. Marta thought the rye was right as it was because her mother had made it that way. That was not a reason, Tobin said. It was the only reason, Marta said, and she said it without heat. The rye went in with the salt she had weighed. Tobin wrote his own number small in the margin of the board, where she would find it later if she looked.

The loaves came out at three. The crust crackled as it met the cooler air. Tobin laid them on the racks in rows of six. The racks were old and the wire sagged in the middle. A cat sat in the window across the street and watched the steam. The street was empty. The lamps along the quay were yellow in the mist.

Dev arrived at four with the van. The engine coughed twice before it stopped. He came in stamping his boots and asking if the kettle was on. The kettle was always on. Marta poured him a cup and set it on the corner of the bench by the till. Dev drank it standing. He had the round sheet folded in his breast pocket.

The round was the same as it had been since the autumn. The hotel on the front took forty rolls and six sandwich loaves. The café by the ferry took the croissants. The fish restaurant took whatever was left of the rye. Dev read the list aloud anyway. Marta liked to hear it. Tobin packed the crates while Dev read.

A crate was short by two rolls. Tobin counted it again and found the same. Marta took two from the rack meant for the shop and put them in without comment. The shop would open with two fewer. Nobody at the counter would count. Dev carried the crates out to the van one by one. The van doors banged in the quiet.

Tobin followed him out with the last crate. The air on the quay smelled of diesel and weed. The tide had turned. Dev stacked the crate against the wheel arch and strapped it down. He asked Tobin whether he meant to stay on after the summer. Tobin said he did not know. Dev nodded as if that was an answer.

Inside, Marta was scraping the bench with the flat blade, working from the far end toward the scale in long strokes that left the wood pale and clean. She paused at the board and read the small number Tobin had written beside the rye. The number was a gram and a half more than hers, and she stood looking at it for a while as the oven ticked behind her. Her mother had never written anything down. Her mother had done it by the palm and by the tongue. Marta took the chalk from the ledge. She did not rub the number out.

The van pulled away up the hill. Tobin came back in with his cheeks red from the wind. He washed his hands at the sink under the slow clock. The window had gone grey. The first gulls were calling over the harbour wall. Marta set the second batch of rye on the bench to rest. She told him to weigh the salt this time.

Tobin weighed it. His hand hovered over the scale for a moment. Then he added the gram and a half and looked at her. Marta was busy with the tins. She did not turn round. The day girls would come at six and find the board full of numbers. They would not know which ones were new, and that suited the two bakers well enough upon a morning like this.

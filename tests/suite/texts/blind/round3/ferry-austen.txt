The ice had come down the river early that year, and the two sisters who kept the ferry at Harrow Bend were therefore obliged to work it by lantern long after the other boats were drawn up. Marta, the elder, held that no crossing should be refused while the channel stayed open.

"We shall lose the whole landing if we turn people away," she said. "They will simply walk to the bridge at Carrow and never come back."

Ilse did not believe that anyone would walk nine miles in January to avoid a ferry. She said so.

"You think everyone is as idle as you are," said Marta, and went on coiling the rope.

It was past six, and dark as a cellar, when the bell on the far post began to ring. Ilse heard it first, because she was nearest the stove and least willing to leave it.

"Somebody wants us."

"Then somebody shall have us." Marta was already lifting the lantern from its hook. "Fetch the pole, and do not look at me like that; I can see your face even in this light."

The flat boat lay low in the black water, with a skin of ice along its gunwale that cracked when they stepped aboard. Ilse took the pole while her sister worked the cable, hand over hand, and the far bank came towards them slowly, as if it were reluctant.

A man stood by the post. He wore a long coat, and he had no bag.

"You are the Varga sisters," he said, before either of them could speak. "I was told you would come."

"Who told you?" asked Ilse.

"A woman at the inn. She said you never refuse anybody, and that I might depend upon it."

Marta gave her sister a look which meant, I told you so. Ilse returned one which meant nothing that could be repeated in company.

"It is a penny each way," said Marta. "Two after dark."

He put four coins into her palm. "I shall want to come back," he said, "though perhaps not tonight."

They pushed off. The current was stronger in the middle, where the ice had not yet taken hold, and the boat swung against its cable with a groan that Ilse felt in her teeth. The stranger did not hold the rail; he stood in the centre of the deck with his hands in his pockets, watching the water as though he expected something to rise out of it.

"Are you from the mill?" Marta asked him.

"No."

"From the railway, then? They say the company means to survey the valley before spring, and consequently we have had a great many gentlemen asking questions."

"I am not a gentleman," he said, "and I have no questions about the railway."

Ilse leaned upon the pole and studied him. He was younger than his voice, and there was something about his mouth that she thought she had seen before, though she could not have said where.

"Then what brings you across?" she said. "There is nothing on our side but the landing, and us, and the churchyard."

He turned and looked at her properly for the first time.

"The churchyard," he said.

Nobody spoke for a while. The lantern hissed. Somewhere upstream a slab of ice broke loose with a sound like a door shutting in an empty house.

"Our mother is buried there," said Marta at last, in the careful tone she used for customers she did not like. "And our father, if you care to know it. You will find the gate is locked at dusk."

"I know it is. I have a key."

"From whom?"

"From him," the stranger said. "From your father. He gave it to me the winter before he died, when I worked this crossing with him, and he told me that if I ever came back I ought to bring it to his daughters. I have been a long time coming back."

Ilse stopped poling. The boat drifted, and Marta had to haul hard on the cable to hold it straight.

"Father never had a boy on the ferry," she said. "I should remember."

"You were away that winter, at your aunt's in Selby. You had a fever, I think." He looked at Ilse. "And you were small, and you were not allowed down to the water at night; so you never saw me, though I saw you at the window every evening."

Ilse remembered the window. She remembered a lantern going out across the river and coming back, and a second shape in the boat beside her father's, which she had always supposed was a dream.

"What is your name?" she asked.

He told her. It meant nothing to Marta, who shook her head; but Ilse repeated it to herself twice, as if she were testing a board before she trusted her weight to it.

The boat touched the landing. Marta tied it off with more force than was needed.

"You may give us the key," she said, "and go back to the inn, and we shall think about whether to believe you."

"That is fair." He drew it out of his coat, a small iron thing on a loop of cord, and laid it in her hand beside the four coins. Then he stepped ashore and stood there, waiting, because he knew perfectly well they would have to take him back.

Ilse looked from the key to her sister's face. It had gone quite still.

"Marta," she said. "It is Father's cord. You plaited it."

"I know I did."

"Then we cannot send him to the inn."

Marta closed her fingers over the key. She was silent so long that the stranger shifted his feet on the frozen planks.

"No," she said finally. "No, I suppose we cannot. Come up to the house; the stove is lit, and my sister has been sitting beside it all evening, so you may be sure it is warm."

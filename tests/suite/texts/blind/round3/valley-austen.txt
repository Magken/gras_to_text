The surveyor arrived at the lip of the valley on a Tuesday, when the water had already covered the lower fields and was beginning to consider the church. He had hired a guide in the village, a woman named Tessaly Marr, who had been recommended to him as the only person still willing to go down.

"You will want the east ridge first," she said. "It is the only road that does not drown."

"I shall want whatever you think best."

She looked at him as if that were an unusual thing for a man with instruments to say. Then she picked up the tripod without asking, and walked.

They went along the ridge until noon. He stopped every few hundred yards to sight the far bank, and she waited with the patience of someone who is paid by the day rather than by the mile.

"What are you measuring, exactly?"

"Where the water is, and where it will be."

"I can tell you that. It is everywhere, and soon it will be everywhere else."

He laughed, because she had not meant it as a joke, and she seemed pleased that he had taken it as one. That was the first day.

The second morning began with fog so thick that he could not see the end of his own chain. She told him it would lift by ten; it lifted at a quarter past, and she apologised for the error with a seriousness that embarrassed him.

"You needn't be sorry. I have known forecasters in the capital who would envy that."

"I am not a forecaster. I grew up there." She pointed downward, into the grey, at a place where he could see nothing but a weathervane standing out of the water like a finger.

He did not know what to say to that. He wrote the bearing of the weathervane in his book, and then, after a moment, he wrote her name beside it.

They took the boat after lunch. It was a flat, patched thing that she had kept tied to a post for exactly this purpose, and she rowed it with a kind of absent competence, as though the oars were simply longer arms.

"That is the mill," she said. "That was the Hallorans' barn. The school is under us now."

"How deep?"

"Deep enough that the bell has stopped."

He lowered his sounding line and counted the marks as they went under. Eleven fathoms, and then the lead touched something that was not mud.

"Roof," she said. "You can write it down."

He wrote it down. Consequently the map, which had been a plain thing of contours and elevations, began to acquire a second layer that nobody in the ministry would ask for: the names of houses, the families who had lived in them, the trees that had marked the boundaries between one field and the next.

"Will they use this?" she asked, watching his pencil.

"They will use the numbers. I do not know what they will do with the rest."

"Then why do you put it in?"

"Because you told me, and it seemed rude not to."

She rowed on in silence for some time. When she spoke again it was about the current, which was turning, and she said that they must go back before it carried them past the narrows; he did not argue, because he had begun to understand that her warnings were never decorative.

That night they camped in the loft of a barn that still stood above the waterline. She lit a small fire in a tin and cooked beans, and he spread his sheets across the boards and inked the day's work by the light of it.

"You draw well."

"I was trained to."

"That is not the same thing." She leaned over and put her finger upon the place where the school had been. "You have made it look as if it is still there."

"It is still there. It is only wet."

She laughed at that, properly, for the first time, and then she stopped, and he saw that her eyes had filled. He looked back at his sheet so that she need not be watched.

The third day was clear and hard and blue. The water had risen another two inches in the night; he measured it against the stake he had driven on the first evening, and she read the figure aloud before he could.

"It will take the church by Sunday."

"Yes, I think it will."

"Then you had better draw the church today."

So they rowed out to it. The nave was already flooded, and the pews had floated up and jammed themselves against the windows, but the tower stood clear, and she climbed it with him to the bell chamber so that he could take his last sightings from the highest point left in the valley.

From there he could see everything he had measured. The ridge, the mill, the weathervane, the brown lake that had been a village: it lay below them like a page he had not yet decided how to finish.

"What will you call it?" she asked.

"The ministry has a number for it."

"I did not ask what they would call it."

He considered this for a long time, longer than the question seemed to deserve, while the wind moved across the water and set the whole surface trembling as if something underneath had turned over in its sleep.

"I shall call it by its name," he said. "If you will tell me how it is spelled."

She told him. He wrote it across the top of the sheet in his best hand, and therefore, when the map was filed in the capital that winter, it bore two titles, one of them a number and the other a word that no clerk could pronounce.

She did not come to the capital. He sent her a copy in the spring, rolled in oilcloth, and some weeks later a letter came back that said only this: "It is very like. Thank you for keeping the bell."

The ferry at the lower bend ran until the ice closed it, and the sisters had run it for eleven winters. Marit worked the cable and Joan kept the book. The book was a ledger with a green cloth spine that had gone black at the corners from the wet. Every crossing went into the book with the hour and the fare and the weight of the load, because the county paid by the crossing. The river was grey that week. The cable sang when the wind came down the valley, and the sound carried to the house where the lamp stayed lit. Joan liked the singing.

The stranger came after dark on the ninth of December. Joan heard the bell on the far post first. The bell was small and the rope was stiff with frost. Marit was at the stove with her boots off. She looked at the window and then at her sister. Nobody crossed after dark. The ferry had no lamp that could be trusted on the water.

The bell rang again. Joan put on her coat and took the lantern from the hook by the door. The yard was white and the path to the landing was white. Across the river a single figure stood by the post with one hand on the rope. The figure did not wave. Joan held the lantern up and the figure held very still, as if the light were a question it meant to answer carefully. It rang a third time.

Marit came down behind her with the cable gloves in her fist, which was how Marit came to most things that frightened her. The two of them stood at the landing and looked across. The water between the banks was black and moving and it carried small plates of ice that turned as they went. A crossing at night was against the rule the county had printed on the card nailed inside the ferry house. Joan knew the rule by heart. She also knew that a person who rang three times on a December night was not asking for the view. The next house upstream was four miles off, and the snow had taken the road. Marit spat on the gloves and pulled them on.

The ferry was a flat deck on two pontoons, and the cable ran from post to post through two iron sheaves on the rail. Marit threw the chock off and leaned. The deck moved out from the landing with the slow reluctance of a thing that had been asleep. Joan stood at the bow with the lantern and watched the ice come at them out of the dark. Each plate knocked against the pontoon and slid under and rose again on the downstream side. The far bank grew until the post was plain and the figure beside it was a man in a long coat. A bag sat at his feet.

"The fare after dark is double," Marit said. The man paid without a word. He paid in coins that were too new. Joan wrote nothing yet. She held the lantern near his face and he let her. The face was young and tired and very clean. The man stepped onto the deck and set the bag down in the middle, where the load was meant to go.

Nobody spoke on the way back. The cable sang. Marit leaned into the gloves and the deck came round against the current. Joan watched the man and the man watched the water. Once he put a hand out toward the bag and then drew it back. The ice was thicker in the middle of the river than it had been an hour before. Joan felt the deck shudder when a large plate struck the upstream pontoon.

The bag moved. It was a small movement and the man saw that Joan had seen it. He did not explain. Marit did not stop leaning. The lantern swung on its handle and the light went over the canvas and back. Something inside the bag had turned over in its sleep. Joan thought of the ledger and the column for the weight of the load.

At the landing the man lifted the bag with both arms. He carried it up the path as though the path were familiar. Joan followed with the lantern. Marit made the ferry fast and came last. In the house the stove had burned low. The man set the bag on the table beside the ledger and opened the neck of it. Inside was a child in a grey blanket.

The child was perhaps two years old. She woke and looked at the lamp. The man said her name was Ellen. He said nothing else about her. Marit put more wood in the stove. Joan sat down at the table and opened the ledger to the page for the ninth. The pen was cold.

The line for each crossing had four columns, and the first three were plain enough because the hour and the fare had been witnessed. The fourth column was for the load. Joan looked at the child and at the man who had carried her over the ice in a canvas bag. She thought of the county clerk who would read the page in spring with his spectacles low on his nose. The clerk would count the passengers against the fares, and the figures had to agree upon the page. Two had crossed and one fare had been paid. Joan wrote one passenger and a bag of ordinary weight.

Marit read the line over her shoulder. She said nothing. The man thanked them both and asked if he might sleep by the stove until light. Marit fetched the second blanket from the chest. The child fell asleep again before the blanket was down. Outside the cable still sang.

In the morning the man and the child were gone, and the coins sat on the table in a small stack. The river had closed in the night. Joan stood at the window and saw that the ice ran from bank to bank without a seam. Nobody would cross on the ferry again until March. She took the ledger down and turned to the ninth. The bag of ordinary weight was there in her own hand. She let the line stand.

It was generally agreed in Aldbury that a church clock which had stood at twenty past four for eleven years could not be expected to start again merely because a wedding had been arranged. Miss Hester Crane, who mended clocks, was of another opinion.

"It shall go by Saturday," she said. "I have promised it."

Her nephew looked up from his tea. "You promised it to whom?"

"To the bride's mother, who stopped me in the lane, and to myself, which is worse."

He put down his cup. He was nineteen, and had come to her that spring because his father thought a trade would steady him; so far it had steadied him chiefly towards her kitchen.

"Then I suppose I am to carry things up the tower."

"You are to carry everything up the tower. I shall carry my opinion."

They went on Monday. The stair was narrow and turned upon itself so often that Tobias declared he had met his own back twice before they reached the chamber. She did not answer him; she was already on her knees before the movement, and she had taken off her gloves.

"Well?" he said.

"It is filthy. It is also perfectly sound."

"Then why does it not go?"

"Because nobody has asked it to, I expect." She drew a finger along the great wheel and showed him the grey upon it. "Eleven years of pigeons and neglect. That is not a disease, Tobias; that is only a bad acquaintance."

He laughed, and she allowed it.

They spent Tuesday taking it apart. She named every piece as she lifted it out, and he wrote each name on a paper label and tied it on with thread, as she told him, because she said a man who trusted his memory in a clock tower deserved whatever it gave him. By evening the floor was laid out like a table after a christening, all small bright things in rows.

"I shall never get them back," he said.

"You will not have to. I shall."

"And what am I for, then?"

"You are for holding the lamp, and for being surprised, which I have given up."

On Wednesday it rained. Water came in beneath the louvres and ran along a beam, and Hester sat upon an upturned bucket and cleaned pivots with a rag and a little oil, and said nothing for nearly an hour. Tobias watched her hands. He had not known that anyone could be so patient with something that could never thank her.

"Aunt," he said at last, "were you ever to be married?"

She did not look up. "Once it was proposed."

"And you refused?"

"I did not refuse. I delayed, and was consequently not asked again, which comes to the same thing and is much less trouble."

He considered that. "I think it was his loss."

"You think so because you are fond of me, and because it is raining, and because you have eaten nothing since noon." She held a pinion to the lamp. "Here. Tell me if you can see a flaw in it."

He could not. She seemed pleased, though whether with the pinion or with him he could not tell.

Thursday was for the escapement. It was the part she liked least and did best; she said it was where a clock decided whether it meant to be honest. She set the pallets by eye and then by ear, bending close, while he held the pendulum and let it go when she told him, and caught it again when she told him, until his arms ached and his temper went.

"How many more times?"

"As many as it takes. Do you want it to be right on Saturday, or right for a week?"

"I want it to be right at all."

"Then let go."

He let go. The pendulum swung, and something in the dark of the frame answered it: a small dry knock, and then another, and then a third that came exactly where she had said it would. Tobias held his breath. Hester only nodded, as if a guest she had been expecting had come to the door.

"There," she said. "Now we must be civil to it."

On Friday they put the hands back and set them by her own watch, which she wound every morning and trusted more than the railway. The bride's mother came up the lane in the afternoon and stood at the foot of the tower, looking up with a hand over her eyes.

"Is it going, Miss Crane?"

"It is going."

"Oh, what a comfort! I told Mr. Pell that you would do it, and he said I was a fool to believe you, and therefore I am doubly glad."

"Tell him it is four minutes past three," said Hester, "and let him check."

On Saturday it struck eleven as the bride came through the gate. Tobias, who stood at the back of the church in a borrowed coat, heard every stroke and counted them, though he knew perfectly well how many there would be. Hester sat in the last pew with her gloves on again. She did not look up at the tower once.

Afterwards, in the churchyard, he found her beside the wall.

"You were not listening," he said.

"I was not listening because I knew." She took his arm. "You may listen for both of us, if you like. It is a thing worth learning, and I cannot do it forever."

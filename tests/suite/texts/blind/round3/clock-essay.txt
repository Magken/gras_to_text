The clock in the tower of St. Aldric had stopped at twenty past four on the Monday, and the vicar sent a boy down the hill with a note before breakfast. Mrs. Vane read the note at the kitchen table and put it under the sugar bowl as though the sugar might keep it from blowing away. The wedding was on the Saturday. The bride was the miller's daughter, and the miller had paid for the bells to be rung at noon exactly, because his own father had married at noon and never let anyone forget the hour. Mrs. Vane packed her tools in the canvas roll. Her nephew Tom carried the lamp.

The stair to the tower was narrow and wet. Tom went first with the lamp held high. Mrs. Vane followed with the roll under her arm and counted the steps aloud. There were ninety-one. At the top a door hung on one hinge, and the room behind it smelled of pigeons and oil. The movement stood in the middle of the floor like a loom. Nobody had touched it in years.

The frame was iron and the wheels were brass. The brass had gone the colour of weak tea. Mrs. Vane walked once around the whole machine and kept her hands in her pockets. Tom asked what was wrong with it. She said that she did not know yet. The not knowing was the first part of the job. Tom had heard this before.

She knelt by the going train and held the lamp close to the escapement, where the pallets met the wheel and one tooth had been bent over like a nail struck crooked. The bent tooth was small, but the whole clock had waited upon it for a week. Tom looked where she pointed and saw nothing at first, then saw it, and felt the odd pleasure of a thing becoming plain. The fault was not the tooth alone, because a tooth does not bend itself and something had been dragging on the train for weeks. Mrs. Vane wrote the date in her book. The book had a green cover and a broken spine.

On the Tuesday they took the movement apart and laid the pieces on a sheet that Mrs. Vane had spread across the boards in the order the clock had taught her. The great wheel went at the top of the sheet, and the pinions went below it in a row like teeth in a jaw. Tom wanted to clean everything at once. Mrs. Vane made him clean one piece and set it down before he picked up the next, because a clean piece among dirty ones was easy to lose. They worked until the light in the louvres went grey. Then they ate bread on the stairs.

The miller came up on the Wednesday. He was a heavy man and he stood in the doorway out of breath. He asked if the clock would be going by Saturday. Mrs. Vane said that it would be going if it was ready. The miller did not like that answer. He looked at the pieces on the sheet as a farmer looks at a field after hail. Then he went down again.

Tom asked why she had not simply promised. Mrs. Vane was polishing a pivot with a scrap of leather. The promise was not hers to make, she said. The clock would decide. A clock that was hurried would run for a month and then fail at a christening. Tom thought about that for some time. The pigeons walked on the slates above them.

The drag was in the motion work behind the dial. On Thursday morning Tom found it himself. A wire from the old bell pull had worked loose and caught against the minute arbor, and each turn of the hands had tugged at the wire a little. Mrs. Vane came and looked. She said nothing for a while. Then she handed him the pliers. It was the first time she had let him cut anything in a church.

The cut was clean. Tom coiled the wire and put it in his pocket to keep. The arbor turned freely under his thumb. Mrs. Vane straightened the bent tooth with a small punch and a great deal of patience. The tooth took nine blows. She counted them the way she had counted the stairs.

On Friday they put the clock back together. The work went slowly and in silence. Each wheel went into its place with a sound like a door closing in another house. Tom held the frame steady while his aunt fitted the pendulum. The pendulum was long and heavy and it swung at last. The escapement began to tick. The tick was uneven at first, and Mrs. Vane knelt and bent the crutch by a hair.

By evening the beat was even, and they sat on the boards and listened to it as other people listen to rain on a roof they have mended. Mrs. Vane set the hands by the watch she carried in her waistcoat, which she had set that morning by the train from the city. Tom asked whether the village would notice any of it. The village would notice only the bells, she said, and nobody would thank them for the bells, because a clock that was right was a clock nobody saw. She wrote the time in the green book. Under the time she left a line empty.

Tom asked what the empty line was for. Mrs. Vane said it was for tomorrow. A repair was not finished until the clock had struck the hour in front of people. She closed the book and put it in her coat. They went down the ninety-one stairs in the dark. The lamp had run out of oil.

On Saturday the square filled before eleven. The bride came up the lane in her mother's dress with the hem pinned. The miller stood at the church door in a collar that was too tight. Tom and his aunt waited at the back by the yew. At noon the hammer lifted and the first bell struck. The miller looked up at the tower. Mrs. Vane took out the green book and wrote one word upon the empty line.

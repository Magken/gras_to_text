At two o'clock the ovens on Quay Street were already warm, and Mrs. Varney had tied her apron twice because the first knot did not please her. Tom came in late.

"You will tell me it was the tide," she said, "and I shall not believe you."

"It was the tide, though. The water was over the steps by the chandler's, and I had to go round by the chapel."

She looked at him as if the chapel were a personal insult. "Then you must leave earlier, since the tide does not consult you."

He hung up his coat and said nothing; it was, he had learned, the most economical answer.

The dough from the evening lay under its cloths along the bench, swollen and pale, like sleepers who had not been told the hour. Mrs. Varney lifted one corner and pressed a finger into it.

"Feel that. Tell me what it wants."

Tom pressed where she had pressed. "Another twenty minutes?"

"Ten. You always give it twenty, because you are frightened of it, and it knows."

"I am not frightened of bread."

"Everyone who has ever ruined a loaf is frightened of bread. I was frightened of it for eleven years."

He did not know whether she was joking, and he had never found a safe way of asking. So he fetched the scales.

They worked for an hour without much talk. The harbour made its small noises outside: a rope knocking against a mast, a gull disputing something with another gull, the long breath of the water against the wall. Inside there was only the scrape of the cutter and the soft slap of each piece turned onto the floured wood.

"Your shaping is better," she said at last. "It is not good, but it is better."

"Thank you."

"I did not say it so that you would thank me. I said it so that you would continue."

At half past three the first trays went in, and the room changed, as it always did, into something warmer and more particular. Tom stood by the ovens with his hands behind his back.

"You needn't guard them," she said. "They are not going to escape."

"I like watching the crust come."

"Then watch it from over there, where you can also wash those bowls."

Pellow arrived at a quarter past four, stamping, with the van left running in the street so that its exhaust drifted past the window in a grey ribbon. He was a large, unhurried man who treated every doorway as a mild inconvenience.

"Morning, Mrs. Varney. Morning, boy."

"He has a name," she said.

"He has. I've heard it. I don't see why I should wear it out." Pellow lowered himself onto the flour sack by the door, which she had asked him a hundred times not to sit upon. "What have you got for the hotel?"

"Four dozen rolls, two sourdough, and the rye they didn't order but will want."

"How do you know they'll want it?"

"Because the new manager is from inland, and inland people always think rye is healthful. Consequently he will ask for it by Thursday, and I would rather he found it already there."

Pellow laughed, a low, crumbling sound. "You ought to be running the hotel."

"I ought to be asleep. That is the only thing I ought to be."

Tom brought out the rolls in their crates and began to count them aloud; Pellow counted with him, a beat behind, which made Tom lose his place twice.

"You're doing that on purpose."

"I am," said Pellow. "It keeps you honest."

"It keeps me slow."

"Honest and slow is a fine combination in a baker. Ask her."

Mrs. Varney did not answer; she was taking the sourdough out, turning each loaf over and knocking on its base with her knuckle, listening as a doctor listens to a chest. One she set aside.

"What's wrong with that one?" Tom asked.

"Nothing that you would notice. Something the hotel would."

"Then who gets it?"

"You do. You shall take it home to your mother, and you shall tell her it was the best of them, which is a lie that will do her no harm."

He was pleased, and tried not to show it, and failed; she saw, and was kind enough to look at the ovens instead.

By five the van was loaded. Pellow stood in the doorway with the cold coming in around him, and the sky over the harbour had begun to turn from black to the colour of wet slate.

"Same again tomorrow?"

"Same again every day, until one of us dies or retires. I have not decided which I shall do first."

"Retire," said Pellow. "You'd hate the other."

When the van had gone, the shop was very quiet. Tom swept the floor while she wrote the night's figures in the book, slowly, in the careful hand of someone who had been taught by a strict woman long ago.

"Mrs. Varney?"

"Yes."

"Were you really frightened for eleven years?"

She put down the pen and considered him, and then the window, where the first gulls were settling on the wall.

"I was frightened until I understood that it would forgive me if I paid attention. Bread is very forgiving, if you attend to it. People are the same, mostly." She picked up the pen again. "Now finish that corner, because you have missed it, and I can see it from here."

The water had come down from the hills in the spring and had not gone back, so the valley lay under one grey sheet. Mr. Lowe reached the ford on the first morning with the chain and the level wrapped in oilcloth. The guide sat upon the wall of the drowned mill with his boots in the water, as if the flood had set him there on purpose. The guide was called Esk. The parish had hired him for three days at a wage that Mr. Lowe thought too high. Esk did not think it high enough. They shook hands without looking at each other, the way two men do when each suspects the other of knowing more than he says.

The boat was flat and black and leaked at the stern. Esk bailed with a tin cup. Mr. Lowe set the level on the bow and found that nothing would stay still. The water moved under them. The hills moved behind the water. He wrote the first reading in his book and drew a line through it. A heron watched them from a drowned hedge.

By noon they had reached the church. Only the tower stood clear. The bell was still in it, and the rope hung down into the nave and vanished. Esk said the sexton had tied it off before he left. Mr. Lowe asked why a man would tie off a bell in a flood. Esk said it was so the wind would not ring it at night. The tower became the first station.

The work of the afternoon was slow, because every mark that Mr. Lowe wanted lay somewhere under the water and had to be found with a pole. The guide knew the valley the way a man knows the inside of his own mouth. He could say where a wall ran or a lane turned by the colour of the reeds above it. Mr. Lowe did not trust this at first. He sounded each place with the lead line and wrote the depth beside the guess. The guesses were good. By the end of the day the two columns in the book agreed more often than they differed, and Mr. Lowe had stopped checking the ones near the road.

They slept that night in the loft of a barn that stood on the only rise for a mile, and the water lapped at the door below them until morning. Esk lit a fire in a bucket. Mr. Lowe asked how long the family had farmed the valley, and Esk said long enough that the question had stopped meaning anything. The surveyor wrote nothing down. He lay awake and thought of the map he would draw, with the clean contours and the careful blue that would show a stranger where the land had gone. The blue would be true. It would also be a kind of lie.

On the second day the rain came back. The boat took on water faster than Esk could bail it, and they put in at the old toll house to wait. The toll board was still nailed beside the door. A cart had cost a penny. A horse had cost a halfpenny. Esk read the prices out loud and laughed at them. Mr. Lowe copied the board into the margin of the book, though it had no place on any map.

The rain stopped in the afternoon. They rowed out to the orchard. The trees stood to their shoulders in the flood, and the apples floated among them like the heads of swimmers. Esk would not let the boat touch the branches. He said the orchard had been his mother's. Mr. Lowe took the readings from the edge and asked nothing more.

The orchard gave him a problem. The book had a column for depth and a column for ground, and a third for the name of the place. Esk had given the orchard a name that was not on the parish plan. Mr. Lowe held the pencil over the column for a long time. Then he wrote the name the guide had said. It was the first word in the book that he had not checked.

That night they did not speak much. The fire in the bucket burned low. Esk mended a rowlock with wire. The surveyor cleaned the lens of the level with the corner of his shirt. Outside, something heavy moved in the water and then went still. Neither man got up to look.

The third morning was clear and cold. The flood had dropped a hand's width in the night, and a line of mud along the barn wall showed it. Esk pointed at the line. Mr. Lowe measured it. A hand's width was not much. It was enough to bring the top of a wall back into the air, and the wall ran straight down the valley toward the ford.

They followed the wall all morning, because the wall was the old boundary between two farms and the parish wanted it fixed before the argument began again. Esk knew both families. He said the wall had been moved twice in his grandfather's time, once by night and once by a lawyer. Neither move had made anyone happier. Mr. Lowe asked which line was the right one. The guide said the right one was the one that the water could not see, and he rowed on before the surveyor could ask what that meant. Mr. Lowe put the wall on the map as it stood.

At the ford they tied the boat to the same gatepost. The chain went back into the oilcloth. Mr. Lowe paid the wage the parish had set, and then a little more from his own pocket. Esk counted it and put it away. They shook hands and this time looked at each other. The heron was on the hedge again.

The map was drawn that winter in a room in the county town. It showed the tower and the toll house and the barn upon the rise, and the long straight wall down the middle. The blue lay over all of it in fine even strokes. In the corner of the orchard there was a small name in pencil that the engraver was told to leave. The engraver asked what it meant. Mr. Lowe said it meant only that someone had told him, and that he had believed it.

The water had come down the valley in March and it had not gone back up. Garrow reached the ford at the south end on the first morning, and the guide was already there with a pole that was longer than the boat he had tied to the willow. His name was Reel and the county had hired him because nobody else would say yes. The map she carried was twelve years old and showed a road, a mill and a church where there was now only brown water with the light lying flat upon it. She asked him where the road went. He pointed at the water. The pole was painted in bands of red and white that Reel had marked himself, and the bands were the only numbers the valley still kept.

They went out in the boat before the mist lifted. Reel stood at the stern and pushed. Garrow sat in the bow with the chain and the level wrapped in oilcloth. The chain was no use on water. She had brought it anyway. The first mark she took was the top of a gatepost that stood a hand above the surface. Reel said the post had belonged to his cousin.

The gatepost went in the book as a fixed point. A fixed point is a thing that stays where it was put. Garrow drew a small cross and wrote the hour beside it. The boat turned slowly while she wrote. Reel held the pole in the mud and waited, and the water moved past the pole with a small sound. She asked what else stood above the flood. He said the chimney of the mill and the bell frame and the elm by the school. That was all.

They reached the mill by noon, and the chimney rose out of the water like a finger that had lost the rest of the hand. Garrow set the level upon the rim of the boat and sighted along it to the bell frame, which stood a mile off and leaned as if the frame had been listening for something below. The reading would not hold because the boat would not hold. Reel pushed the pole deeper and leaned his whole weight against it. The bubble in the level crept to the middle and stayed there for the length of a breath. She wrote the number down before it could change its mind. Reel let go and the boat drifted.

On the second day the wind came up from the east and pushed the water into small ridges that slapped against the side of the boat all morning. Garrow did not take readings in the wind, so she took soundings instead, and Reel called out each depth from the bands on the pole in a voice that did not rise or fall. Four red. Three white. Four red again. The numbers went into the book in a column that ran down the whole page and onto the next one. Under the boat were fields that Reel had walked as a boy, and he named each one as they passed over it, and the names went in the margin because there was no other place for them.

The names were not part of the survey. Garrow knew that. The county wanted the depth and the line of the old road and the height of the water at the church. It did not want Low Acre or the Priest's Meadow or the field where the Dunns had kept a bull. She wrote the names small. Reel watched her write them and said nothing about it. The silence was a courtesy, or it was the cold, and Garrow let the question alone.

At the church they tied the boat to the bell frame. The bell was gone. Somebody had taken it out in the first week, and Reel said it was in a barn up on the ridge with hay packed around it. Garrow measured from the water to the crossbeam. The crossbeam had a date cut into it. Reel put his thumb over the date and then took the thumb away. The water was eleven feet above the church floor.

They ate on the boat because there was nowhere dry to land. Reel had bread and a hard cheese and he cut the cheese with the same knife he used for the rope. Garrow ate and looked at the book. The book was swollen at the edges from the damp. The line of the road ran across two pages and stopped at the church. After the church the road went nowhere she could find.

On the third morning the water had dropped by the width of a band. Reel saw it first. He showed her the wet line on the gatepost above the surface. Garrow took the reading again from the same fixed point. The number was different, and the difference ran through every number that came after it. She would have to correct the whole book. Reel asked if that was bad.

Garrow said it was only arithmetic. Arithmetic was the easy part. The hard part was the road, and the road was still under the water where the map said it ought to be. Reel said he knew where it went. He had driven cattle along it every autumn until the flood. He took the boat across the valley in a slow curve and stopped over each bend. At each bend he pushed the pole down until it struck stone.

Garrow sounded every bend that Reel found, and by the middle of the afternoon the old road lay across her page in a line of small crosses that curved the way a river curves. It did not match the county map at all. The county map had the road running straight past the mill, as if the man who drew it had looked at the valley from the ridge and guessed. Reel studied the page over her shoulder for a long time. Then he pointed at a gap between two crosses where the road dipped to the ford that his grandfather had paved. She sounded it and wrote the depth. The line was whole.

They came back to the willow as the light went. Reel tied the boat and stood on the bank with the pole across his shoulders. Garrow paid him from the county purse and he counted the coins twice. She asked if he would stay in the valley when the water went down. He said the water would not go down this year. He said it the way a man reports the weather.

Garrow walked up to the ridge road with the book inside her coat. The names were in the margin and the crosses were on the page and the date from the crossbeam was nowhere at all. She had not written it down. At the top she turned and looked back. The valley was one grey sheet with the chimney standing out of it. Somewhere under the sheet the road went on curving toward the ford, and the county would have its map by Friday, and Friday would be dry.

The surveyor came into the valley on a Tuesday, when the water had stood a fortnight in the lower fields, and he hired the first person at the inn who claimed to know the drowned roads.

"You will want the old lanes," said the woman, "because the new ones were never worth much, and now they are under six feet of river."

"I want whatever can be measured," he told her. "My employers are particular about that."

She laughed at him, not unkindly. "Then you shall be disappointed by noon, sir, for nothing here holds still long enough to be measured."

Her name was Ann Treloar, and she had kept sheep on the eastern slope until the flood took the fold. He offered her three days; she asked for four, and he gave in with a reluctance that amused her.

On the first morning they went out in a flat boat borrowed from the miller. He sat in the bow with his chain and his book, and she poled them between the tops of hedges.

"That was Pascoe's orchard," she said. "You may put it down as water, if your employers prefer."

"I shall put it down as an orchard under water. It is a distinction they will understand."

"Will they pay him for his apples, then?"

He did not answer. The question seemed to him impertinent, and he suspected that she meant it to be.

By afternoon he had fixed four stations on the higher ground, and she had shown him where the church path ran beneath the flood, marked only by a line of stunted willows. He was glad of her, though he did not say so; he thought it would make her ask for a fifth day.

"You write very small," she observed, looking over his shoulder as they drifted.

"The paper is dear, and the valley is large."

"The valley was never large until it filled. Before that you could walk across it in an hour and be home for dinner."

That evening at the inn he drew the first sheet by candlelight, and it troubled him that the river on his paper looked so orderly. He had given it a clean edge where there was no edge at all.

The second day was wet. They went up the western side on foot, because the boat had sprung a seam in the night, and she led him along the ridge through bracken that soaked him to the waist.

"Consequently you will catch cold," she said, "and everybody in the parish will blame me for it."

"Nobody in the parish knows me."

"They know you now. You are the man who counts the water. Mrs. Rowe believes you have come to take the land away from us, and I did not contradict her, as it seemed a pity to spoil her afternoon."

He was not pleased by this, and he told her so; she only shrugged and went on ahead.

From the ridge he could see the whole flood at once, grey and flat and patient, with the chimneys of three farms standing out of it like the posts of a pier. He took his bearings from each chimney in turn. She named them for him as he worked.

"Hendra. Polglaze. That last one was my brother's, before he went to the mines."

"Is he there still?"

"He is somewhere. He writes at Christmas, if he remembers, and he never remembers."

It was the only thing she told him about herself, and he wrote it nowhere, though he found that he did not forget it.

In the afternoon the rain thinned. They came down by a sheep track to the edge of the water, where a gate stood upright in the flood with nothing on either side of it.

"I had that gate made," she said. "It cost me eleven shillings, and it was the finest gate in the valley."

"It still is, I suppose."

"It is the only gate in the valley. That is not the same thing, and you know it."

On the third morning the water had fallen by a hand's breadth, and a strip of mud showed along every shore like a tide line. He measured it with care. She watched him, and for once she was quiet.

"If it keeps falling," he said at last, "your fields will be dry by the spring, and my map will be wrong."

"Then you had better draw it quickly, sir, while it is still true."

He laughed, which surprised them both. After that the work went easily; she held the staff where he told her, and he read the levels aloud, and she repeated each figure back to him as if it were a line of a hymn.

They finished at the gate. He closed his book upon the last station and sat for a while on the gunwale of the mended boat, with the cold coming up off the water.

"What will they do with it?" she asked.

"They will decide whether a dam can be built at the head of the valley. If it can, the water will stay."

She looked at him a long time, and he did not look away, because he thought he owed her that much at least.

"You might have told me on the first day," she said.

"I might. I did not think you would guide me, if I did."

"I would have charged you double, and guided you all the same." She stood and took up the pole. "Somebody ought to see it properly before it goes, and therefore it had better be you than nobody."

He paid her for four days at the inn that night. She took the money without counting it, and she did not wish him a good journey; but in the morning, when he looked back from the pass, she was standing at the water's edge, and she lifted her hand.

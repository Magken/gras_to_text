The bakery on Quay Street lit its ovens at ten, and the glass of the front window went grey with steam before the last pub had closed. Tamsin Rowe came down the back stairs with the key on a string around her wrist. The apprentice was already at the bench. Lew had tied his apron twice because the first knot had come loose on the stairs. The flour bins stood in a row along the wall, and each lid had a name chalked upon it in the baker's square hand. The rye was on the left. The white was on the right, where the lamp threw the most light across the scoop.

Tamsin looked at the dough that had risen overnight in the cold room. The dough was soft at the top and tight at the edge. Tamsin pressed a thumb into it and watched the dent. The dent filled slowly. That was the sign. Lew watched the thumb and not the dough. The baker told him to watch the dough.

The mixer was old and it shook the floor when it ran. Lew fed the mixer water from a jug marked in pencil. The pencil marks had faded to nothing near the bottom. Tamsin knew the lines by the sound of the pour. Lew did not know them yet. The baker said nothing about it. A thing like that came with the nights, or it did not come.

At midnight the harbour went quiet, and the only sound on the street was the rigging of the moored boats tapping against the masts in the wind. The first trays went into the deck oven at a quarter past. Tamsin set the timer and then ignored it, because the timer had been wrong for three winters and the crust was never wrong. Lew asked how the baker could tell. Tamsin opened the door a finger's width and let the apprentice smell the bread. The smell was sweet and a little burnt at the edge, the way a match smells after the flame has gone out. Lew nodded as if he understood.

The delivery driver came in at two with the cold still on her coat and a crate of empty baskets resting on her hip. Mrs. Coyle had driven the same van for eleven years, and the van had a dent in the side door that the driver would not let anyone fix. Mrs. Coyle said the dent was how the café owners knew the bread had arrived. Tamsin said the café owners knew by the bread. The argument was an old one, and both women liked the argument too much to finish it. Mrs. Coyle set the baskets down by the door. Lew took them without being told.

The loaves came out in a long row. Tamsin tapped the bottom of each loaf with a knuckle. The good ones sounded hollow. One sounded dull. The baker set the dull one aside. Lew asked what was wrong with it. Tamsin said the loaf had been too near the back wall. The apprentice wrote that down in the notebook he kept in his apron.

The notebook was small and blue and bent from the heat. Lew had filled half the notebook in a month. The pages held oven times and weights and the names of the cafés. Some pages held only a question. Mrs. Coyle read one over the apprentice's shoulder and laughed. The question was why the rye took longer. Tamsin answered it without looking up.

The rye had less in it that would rise. That was the whole of the answer. Lew waited for more. There was no more. The baker had learned the answer from her father at the same bench, and the father had given the same answer to the same question. Mrs. Coyle poured tea from the pot on the shelf.

The van was loaded by three. The cafés on the front got the white loaves and the rolls. The hotel on the hill got the rye and a box of the small sweet buns. Mrs. Coyle counted the crates twice and signed the sheet. The sheet went on a nail by the door. The nail had held the same kind of sheet for as long as the bakery had stood.

Lew carried the last crate out to the street. The air was wet with salt. A gull stood on the roof of the van and did not move for the apprentice. Mrs. Coyle clapped her hands once. The gull went. The driver climbed into the cab and wound the window down.

Mrs. Coyle told the apprentice that the baker had once burnt a whole night of bread because a boat had come in late with her brother on it. Lew had not heard that. Tamsin had never told the story, and the driver said the silence was upon purpose, because a baker who admitted to one burnt night would be asked about all of them. The van pulled away from the kerb with its one bad headlight. Lew stood in the street until the light turned the corner by the fish market and the harbour was dark again. Then he went back inside.

Tamsin was scraping the bench with a flat blade. The flour came up in curls. The baker did not ask what the driver had said. Lew took the broom from the corner and swept the floor under the racks. The broom had lost half its bristles. The floor was clean anyway.

At five the first light came grey over the harbour wall. The baker turned the sign on the door. A man in boots bought two rolls and ate one on the step. Lew watched the man through the window. The dull loaf was still on the side, and Tamsin broke the loaf open and gave the apprentice half. The inside was fine. The apprentice wrote that down too.

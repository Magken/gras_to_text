The ferry at the lower bend of the river ran on a cable that the sisters greased each morning with fat from the kitchen pot. Maren was the elder and kept the ledger, and Ilse kept the pole and the rope and the iron bell that hung from the post on the near bank. The river froze at the edges first and left a black lane down the middle that the ferry cut open again every day until the ice gave up. The ledger had a line for the name and a line for the fare and a line for the cargo. Maren wrote slowly. The ferry did not cross after dark, because the ice shifted at night and the cable made a sound that neither sister liked to hear.

The rule was their father's. The father had drowned in a March thaw with the rule still in his mouth. Ilse did not speak of the thaw. Maren spoke of the thaw only when the ledger was closed and the lamp was low. The house stood above the landing on four stone feet. The kitchen window looked down upon the bell.

On the night of the first hard frost the bell rang once. Ilse was at the stove. Maren was at the table with the ledger open. The bell rang again, and the second ring was slower than the first. Ilse put down the spoon. Maren closed the ledger and did not stand.

The stranger at the post was a tall man in a grey coat who held the bell rope as if the rope might pull him somewhere he did not wish to go. Ilse went down the path with the lantern and Maren came after her with the ledger under her arm, because the ledger went wherever a fare might be. The man said that he had to reach the far bank before morning. The man did not give a name. Ilse lifted the lantern to see the face, and the face was young and chapped and very tired. The far bank was a dark line with one light in it. Maren said that the ferry did not cross at night and that the rule was older than either of them.

The man took a coin from the coat and held the coin out on his open palm so that the lantern fell upon it and made it look like more than it was. Ilse looked at the coin and then at the river, where the black lane had already gone grey at the edges and would be white by the small hours. Maren looked at the man. The quarrel between the sisters had been going on since the thaw, and each of them knew the next word before the other said it. Ilse said the cable was sound. Maren said that the cable had been sound in March.

The man waited. The coin stayed on the palm. Ilse set the lantern on the post and began to unwind the rope from the cleat. Maren did not stop her. Maren opened the ledger instead and wrote the date at the top of a clean line. The pen was stiff with cold.

"Your name," Maren said. The man said a name that might have been true. Maren wrote the name down. In the fare column Maren wrote the coin. In the cargo column Maren wrote nothing, because the man carried nothing but the coat. Ilse pushed off from the landing with the pole.

The ferry moved out into the lane. The cable hummed above them. The ice at the edges knocked against the hull with a sound like knuckles on a door. Ilse leaned on the pole and the pole bent. The man stood in the middle of the deck with his hands in the pockets of the coat. Maren stood at the stern and watched the near bank shrink.

Halfway across the cable changed its note. Ilse heard the change first. Ilse stopped poling and looked up at the line. The man looked up too. The note climbed and held. Nobody moved.

"Keep on," Maren said. Ilse kept on. The ferry slid forward a length and then another. The note fell back to a hum. The far light grew into a window with a woman behind the glass. The woman did not wave.

The man stepped onto the far landing before the ferry had fully stopped, and the man turned back once with the lantern light on him and said something that the wind took away before it reached the deck. Ilse called after him but the man was already on the path and climbing toward the lit window where the woman stood without moving. Maren watched the door of that house open and close upon the two of them. The window went dark a moment later. The sisters were alone on the river with a coin and a line in the ledger that nobody would ever check. Ilse said the rule had been wrong. Maren said that the rule had been right and the sisters had been lucky, and the two were not the same.

The crossing back was quiet. The cable kept its hum. The ice had closed a little behind the ferry and Ilse broke the ice again with the pole. Maren held the lantern. The house above the landing was dark because the stove had gone out. Ilse tied the rope to the cleat with a double knot.

In the kitchen Maren opened the ledger on the table and looked at the line. The name was there and the coin was there. The cargo column was still empty. Maren could have written the woman in the window into that column, or the note of the cable, or the father. Maren wrote nothing. The empty space was the only true thing on the page, and Maren left the space open on purpose.

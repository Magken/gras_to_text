The water had come down the valley in the second week of March, and by April it lay across the lower fields like a sheet of tin. Mr. Ashby arrived on the carrier's cart with a chain, a level, and a case of ruled paper that the county had paid for. The guide was a man called Tobin Reed who had farmed the east slope until the river took the barn and the two cows inside the barn. Tobin met the cart at the bridge. The bridge was half under. Mr. Ashby looked at the brown water moving through the rails and asked whether the valley had any ground left that a man could stand upon. Tobin said it had some.

They began on the first morning at the churchyard wall. The wall was the highest stone in the parish. Mr. Ashby set the level upon the coping and sighted down toward the mill. Tobin held the staff. The staff was painted in red and white bands. Every band that went under meant a foot of water over the old road. The surveyor wrote the numbers in a clean hand.

By noon the numbers had a pattern. The water was deepest at the mill and shallowest by the smithy, though the smithy stood lower on every map. Tobin said the maps were wrong. Mr. Ashby asked how he knew. Tobin pointed at the chimney of the smithy, which stood dry above a roof of wet thatch. The chimney had been built on a knoll. The knoll was not on any sheet.

In the evening they sat at the inn with the sheets spread over two tables, and the landlord's daughter brought candles because the lamps had drowned with the cellar. Mr. Ashby drew the line of the water in blue pencil and the line of the old roads in black, so that the two lines crossed and parted like the threads of a bad weave. Tobin watched the pencil for a long while. Then the guide put a finger on a blank place near the river. His house had been there. The surveyor asked whether Tobin wanted it marked. Tobin said a map was for the county and the county did not need his house.

The second day was colder, and a mist sat upon the flood so thickly that the staff vanished at forty yards and came back only when Tobin waved it. Mr. Ashby worked by sound for most of the morning, calling the bands out loud and waiting for the guide to answer from the white. The answers came slow. A heron rose once between them and neither man saw the heron land. By noon the surveyor had filled four sheets with figures that he did not trust, because the mist bent the sight line in ways the printed tables could not correct. He kept the figures anyway. A doubtful number was still a number.

In the afternoon they took the punt. Tobin poled it along the line of a drowned hedge. The hedge showed as a row of black twigs above the water. Mr. Ashby sounded the depth at every tenth pole with a weighted cord. The cord came up wet to the knot and then past the knot. Near the mill the punt struck something soft. Tobin did not look down.

The surveyor wrote nothing for that sounding. He left the space in the column blank. Tobin saw the blank and said nothing about it. They went on to the mill race. The race was loud with water that had nowhere else to go. Mr. Ashby took the last reading there and the light went.

That night the landlord asked what the map was for. Mr. Ashby said the county meant to cut a new channel below the mill. The channel would drain the lower fields by summer. The landlord said the lower fields had been drained before, in his grandfather's time. The river had come back then too. Tobin laughed at that, once. The laugh was the first the surveyor had heard from him.

On the third morning the mist was gone. The valley lay open and bright under a clean sky. Mr. Ashby could see the whole flood from the churchyard wall, and the flood was smaller than his sheets had said. Tobin said the water always looked smaller in sun. The surveyor began to correct the figures. Some of them moved by a foot. Some did not move at all.

They walked the east slope in the afternoon. The ground there was firm and the grass had begun to green. Tobin led the way to a line of stones that had been a wall. Beyond the wall the ground fell to the river. A doorstep sat at the edge of the water with nothing behind it. The step was scrubbed white.

Mr. Ashby set up the level upon the doorstep because it was the only flat stone for a hundred yards, and Tobin held the staff down at the waterline without being asked. The surveyor read the bands and wrote the figure and then wrote it again in the margin, the way a clerk writes a sum that he means to keep. The step stood eleven feet above the river. That was the height the flood had not reached. Mr. Ashby asked whether the house had stood there long. Tobin said his father had laid the step and his father's father had cut the stone for it from the quarry on the ridge. The surveyor wrote that down too.

The cart came for him at dusk. Tobin carried the chain to the bridge. The bridge was clear of water now by the width of a hand. Mr. Ashby paid the guide in silver and the guide counted it twice. They shook hands at the rail. Neither man spoke of the punt.

On the road north the surveyor opened the case and looked at the sheets. The blue line and the black line crossed as before. Near the river there was a small square that had not been there in the county's copy. Mr. Ashby had drawn it in the last light without deciding to. The square had no name beside it. The county would ask what it was. He thought he would tell them it was a doorstep.

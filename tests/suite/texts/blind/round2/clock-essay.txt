The clock in the tower of the church at Hurle had stopped at twenty past four on a Sunday in May, and nobody in the village could say which Sunday it had been. Ada Venn came up from the valley on the Monday with a leather case of tools and her nephew Tom, who carried the lamp and the oil and a loaf wrapped in a cloth. The wedding was on the Saturday. The vicar met them at the lych gate and spoke about the bride as if the bride were a part of the mechanism that also needed seeing to. Ada listened. Tom looked up at the dial, which was blue and gilt and flaking, and the hands stood where the hands had stood for a year. The vicar said the bells must ring the hour when the couple came out of the porch.

The stair to the clock room was narrow. Ada went first with the lamp. The room smelled of pigeons and old grease. The movement sat in an iron frame upon a bench of oak, and the pendulum hung still in its slot. Tom set the loaf on the sill. Ada put a hand on the great wheel and did not turn it. "Not yet," she said.

The first day was for looking. Ada drew the train of wheels in a notebook with a soft pencil. The going train went on the left page. The striking train went on the right. Tom held the lamp where she pointed and learned to keep his shadow off the page. By the evening the drawing had forty teeth on the scape wheel and a question mark beside the fly. The question mark stayed there all night.

On the Tuesday Ada took the movement down to its plates, and Tom laid each part on a clean sheet in the order that it came away, because Ada had told him that the order was the only map a clock would ever give. The pivots were brown with old oil that had gone to varnish. Ada cleaned the pivots in a saucer of paraffin and polished them with a burnisher that had belonged to her father. Tom asked why the oil had turned. Ada said that oil kept faith for about ten years if a person was lucky, and the last person to oil this clock had not been lucky or had not come back. The saucer went black. Tom changed the paraffin twice.

The quarrel between them began on the Wednesday over the pallets, which were worn into two small cups where the scape wheel had struck them for a hundred years or more. Tom wanted to turn the pallets round and use the fresh faces, as a cousin of his had done with a mill clock in the next valley. Ada wanted to dress the faces with a stone and keep the old geometry that the maker had cut. The quarrel had come up before, and each knew that Ada would win it. The stone was grey and fine. Ada worked the pallets for an hour while Tom sulked at the window and watched the florist's cart come up the lane with a load of white stock for the porch. Then Tom came back to the bench.

A girl brought tea up the stair at four. The girl was the bride's sister and she wanted to see the clock. Ada showed her the wheels on the sheet. The girl counted the wheels and asked which one was the heart. Tom pointed to the scape wheel. Ada pointed to the weight. The girl laughed and took the cups away.

The weight was a drum of lead the size of a churn. The rope that held it had rotted where it lay across the barrel. Tom found the rot with his thumb. Ada cut the rope back to sound fibre and spliced in a length of new hemp from the chandler in the town. The splice was ugly but it was strong. Tom said so. Ada said a splice was meant to be strong and nothing else.

On the Thursday the clock went back together. Ada set each wheel by the drawing, and Tom read the teeth aloud from the notebook. The plates closed. The pins went in. Ada hung the pendulum and gave the bob a push with one finger. The clock ticked. The tick was uneven, a long beat and a short one, like a man with a stone in his boot.

Tom wanted to shift the crutch at once. Ada made him wait and listen. The clock limped for ten minutes. Then Ada knelt and bent the crutch a hair's width with a pair of brass pliers. The beat came even. Tom let out a breath he had held. Ada wrote the time in the notebook and the words in beat, and nothing more.

The striking train was harder. The hammer rose and fell but the count went wrong at the third hour. The bell gave five for three. Down in the lane a man with a ladder looked up and shook his head. Tom went red. Ada opened the notebook to the question mark beside the fly.

The fly was a small fan of brass that spun as the bell struck and held the strokes to a measured pace, and a burr on the arbour had let it catch and slip so that the count wheel ran ahead of itself. Ada found the burr by drawing a fingernail along the arbour in the dark, because the lamp showed nothing and a nail showed everything. Tom held the lamp anyway. Ada took the burr off with a file no longer than a matchstick and oiled the arbour with a single drop from a needle. The bell struck three at the next trial, and then four at the hour after, and the man in the lane took his ladder away without looking up. Ada rubbed out the question mark. Tom asked to keep the page.

The Friday was for watching. Ada set the hands by the railway clock in the town and came back up the hill on foot. The clock gained a minute by noon. Ada moved the rating nut a quarter turn. By the evening the clock kept time with her watch. Tom slept on a sack in the clock room so the clock would not be alone.

On the Saturday the couple came out of the porch at a quarter to twelve and stood in the white stock while the photographer counted. Ada and Tom stood by the yew with their arms folded. The minute hand reached the top of the dial. The hammer lifted. The bell struck twelve and did not miss a stroke. Nobody in the churchyard looked up at the tower. The notebook was in Ada's coat, and the page with the rubbed-out mark was in Tom's.

The ferry at the bend of the river ran on a cable that the sisters greased each morning before the ice could take it. Ruth was the elder and she kept the ledger, and Anna kept the engine, which was older than either of them and had never once been called by a name. The water was black in December. It moved under the planks with a low sound that the sisters had stopped hearing years ago, the way a woman stops hearing the clock in her own kitchen. The landing had a lamp on a post. The lamp was lit at four and put out at seven. After seven there was no crossing, because the river did not forgive a mistake made in the dark.

The rule was their father's. He had written it on a board and nailed the board to the shed. The paint had flaked. Anna had painted it over twice. Ruth said the words did not need paint. They needed only to be kept.

On the night of the stranger the lamp was already out. Anna was in the shed with a rag and a can of oil. Ruth was counting coins into a tin. The count was short by a single fare. She counted again and got the same number. She did not like a short count.

The knock came on the shed door at a quarter past eight, and it was a patient knock, the knock of a man who expected to wait and had decided that waiting was part of the price. Anna looked at Ruth, and Ruth looked at the board on the wall as if it might speak for her. The man outside said that he needed to cross. He said it plainly. He had a coat too thin for the season and a bag that he held against his chest with both arms, as a child holds a cat that wants to be put down. His boots were wet to the ankle. Ruth told him the ferry was closed.

He did not argue with her, and that was the thing Anna remembered afterward, because every man who had come late to the landing before him had argued, and had offered more money, and had spoken of a sick mother or a missed train. This one only nodded. He asked if he might sit by the stove until morning. Ruth said he might. She gave him the stool by the door and a cup of the coffee that had stood on the stove since noon, which was bitter enough to wake the dead and hot enough to make the dead regret it. He drank it without a word. Then he set the bag down at his feet.

The bag was leather and very old. Something inside it was heavy. Anna watched it the way she watched a gauge. The man saw her watching. He did not open it. He said only that it had to be on the far side by dawn.

Ruth went back to her tin. The count was still short. She wrote the number in the ledger and drew a line under it. The line was straighter than it needed to be. Anna knew what that meant. Her sister was thinking.

The far side was a village of nine houses and a church with no priest. Nobody went there in winter who did not have to. The sisters knew every family by the shape of its lamps. They knew who was ill. There was an old woman in the house by the church who had been dying since October, slowly, as if she meant to do it well. Anna said her name out loud.

The man looked up. He said that the woman was his mother. He said it the way people say the time. Ruth put her pen down. She asked him why he had not come at four. He said the bus had broken down on the hill road, and he had walked.

Anna went to the window. The river was moving fast under a thin skin of ice. The cable was a dark line against the snow. She could see the far landing only because she knew where it was. Her father had crossed at night once. He had come back with his hands torn and had never spoken of it.

Ruth stood and took the lamp from its hook, and she lit it with a match from the tin, and her hands did not shake, though Anna could see that they wanted to. She said that the rule was a good rule and that it had kept them alive, and that a rule which could not bend for a dying woman was not a rule but a stone that someone had set upon the road. Anna said nothing to that. She went out to start the engine. It coughed twice and caught on the third pull, which was its habit in the cold. The man followed with his bag. Ruth came last.

The crossing took eleven minutes. Nobody spoke. The ice broke against the bow with a sound like plates. Anna kept her eyes on the cable. Ruth held the lamp high. The man sat in the middle with the bag on his knees.

On the far side he paid the fare. It was one coin, and it was exactly the coin the tin had lacked. Ruth did not remark upon it. She put it in her pocket and they turned back toward the lamp on the post, which Anna had left burning. The count would be right in the morning. That was enough.

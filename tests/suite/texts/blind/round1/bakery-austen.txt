The oven on Quay Street had been lit at ten, and by eleven Mr. Hale was already complaining that it would never be hot enough.

"It is the damp," he said to his apprentice. "The harbour creeps under the door and sits in the bricks."

Nell did not answer him. She had heard the same remark every night since March, and she thought it wiser to flour the bench.

"You might at least agree with me," he said.

"I agree that you say it, Mr. Hale."

He laughed at that, because he liked her better when she was impertinent; it meant she was awake. He took the first tub of dough from the proving shelf and turned it out with a sound like a wet sail falling.

The clock above the flour bins said a quarter past eleven when the bell rang, although no customer had any business there at that hour. It was Tomas, the driver, who came early whenever his wife had put him out of temper.

"Is there tea?" he asked.

"There is tea for those who carry sacks," said Mr. Hale.

"Then I shall carry a sack." He carried two, and set them down by the mixer with more ceremony than they deserved; afterwards he sat upon the step and drank his tea as though it had cost him a fortune.

Nell divided the dough by weight. She was quick at it now, and the scale hardly troubled her; she could feel four hundred grams in her palm before the needle had settled.

"She is getting too good," Tomas said. "You will lose her to the hotel."

"The hotel does not want her," said Mr. Hale. "The hotel buys its rolls frozen from a lorry, and consequently it has no need of anyone who can shape a loaf."

"I might want the hotel," said Nell.

"You might want a great many things that are bad for you."

She shaped the loaves in silence after that. Outside, the fishing boats knocked against the pontoon, and a gull complained somewhere above the chandlery in a voice not unlike her master's.

Midnight came without anyone noticing it. The first batch went in; the oven door closed upon it with a heavy, satisfied click.

"Now we wait," said Mr. Hale.

"You always say that," said Tomas, "as if it were a discovery."

"It is a discovery, every night. You would not believe how many people never learn it."

Tomas put down his cup. He had a way of looking at the baker that was half affectionate and half despairing, as a man looks at a clock that is always ten minutes slow but which he cannot bring himself to replace.

"My wife says I should find work in the daytime," he said.

"Your wife is very sensible."

"She says a man who delivers bread at four in the morning has no conversation at dinner."

"Does she want conversation at dinner?"

"She wants it more than she wants me, I think."

Nell looked up at this, because it was the first honest thing Tomas had said in a month. But Mr. Hale only nodded, as if he had expected it all along, and handed the driver a heel of yesterday's rye with the butter already spread.

"Eat that," he said. "It will improve your temper, if not your marriage."

By two the shop smelled of crust and caramel, and the windows had steamed so thickly that the harbour lamps looked like coins dropped in milk. Nell opened the back door to let the heat escape. The air came in cold and salty; it smelled of diesel, and weed, and the particular rot of rope left too long in the water.

"Shut that," said Mr. Hale. "You will ruin the croissants."

"The croissants are not in yet."

"Then you will ruin my anticipation of them."

She shut it. She did not mind, really; there was a comfort in being scolded by someone who would also, at half past three, give her the first pain au chocolat from the tray without a word.

The second batch went in at twenty to three. Tomas began to load the crates into the van, counting aloud as he went, and every so often losing his place and starting again from the beginning.

"Nineteen for the café on the corner," he said. "Twelve for the guesthouse. Four for Mrs. Pryce, who will say that she ordered six."

"She ordered four," said Nell.

"She will say six. That is the arrangement between us."

Mr. Hale wiped his hands upon his apron and stood for a moment at the window, although there was nothing to see. The street was empty. The boats were dark, and only the lighthouse at the end of the breakwater kept turning, patient and indifferent, as it had done since before any of them were born.

"Do you ever think of stopping?" Nell asked him.

"Every night," he said. "Around one o'clock."

"And then?"

"And then the bread comes out."

That was all the answer she received. She thought it was a poor one at the time, and it was only years later, in a kitchen of her own, that she understood it had been the only answer he possessed.

At four Tomas drove away with the van full and the heater broken, singing badly. The baker and the apprentice stood in the doorway and watched his tail lights go round the bend by the fish market.

"He will not leave," said Mr. Hale. "Whatever his wife says."

"How do you know?"

"Because he came early."

Nell considered this. She turned it over as she had turned the dough, feeling for the weight of it, and found that it was right.

Behind them the oven ticked as it cooled. The sky over the water had begun, very slightly, to thin.

"Put the kettle on," said Mr. Hale. "We have the morning to do yet."

The bakery on Quay Street lit its ovens at ten, and the smell of them went down the hill to the water before anyone else in the town was awake. Mr. Voss came in by the back door with his collar up and his keys already in his hand, because he did not like to stand in the lane and search his pockets. Nell was there before him. She had swept the floor and set out the tins in rows that were straighter than they needed to be. He looked at the rows and said nothing about them. The flour sacks leaned against the wall like men who had been told to wait. It was a quiet start.

The first dough was the brown loaf. Voss weighed the salt himself. He trusted Nell with the water and the yeast but not yet with the salt. She knew this and did not mind it. The scale had a brass pan that had gone dark at the edges. He tipped the salt into the bowl and wiped the pan with his thumb. Then the mixer began.

The harbour was black beyond the window. A single boat had its lamp lit at the end of the pier. Nell watched it while the arm of the mixer turned. She wondered who was out there at this hour and what they were waiting for. Voss told her to watch the dough instead. The boat did not care whether she watched it.

By midnight the brown loaves were proving in their tins under cloths, and the room had grown so warm that Nell rolled her sleeves above the elbow and tied back her hair a second time. Voss began the white dough, which was the one he would not hurry, because the customers who bought it early had bought it from his father and knew the difference in the crumb. He worked it by hand on the long bench. The bench had a dip in the middle where three generations of palms had pressed. Nell stood at the far end and cut the rolls. Her knife was quick. His hands were slow and they were never wrong, and she had begun to understand that the slowness was the part that nobody could teach her.

Mr. Tarrant came at two with the van, as he did every night except Sunday, and he left the engine running in the lane so that the whole back wall shook a little. He was a large man who wore a woollen hat in every season and who spoke to the bread as though it could hear him. He put his head round the door and asked if the rolls were ready. They were not. Voss told him to come in and shut the door, because a draught would sink the white loaves if it caught them on the bench. Tarrant came in and stood by the ovens with his hands held out to them. Nell poured him a cup of tea from the pot on the shelf.

The tea was stewed. Tarrant drank it anyway. He said the café by the ferry had asked for more croissants. Voss said the café by the ferry could ask. It was an old quarrel and both of them enjoyed it. Nell kept her eyes on the rolls. She had learned that these talks went better without her.

The first oven opened at half past two. Heat came out of it in a slab. Voss drew the brown loaves on the long peel and turned them onto the rack. He tapped the bottom of each with his knuckle. A hollow note meant done. A dull one meant back in. Only one went back.

Nell counted the loaves into the crates for the van. Each crate had a name chalked on its end. The hotel took twelve. The café took eight and would take more if it were given more. The chapel hall took four on a Friday for reasons nobody had explained. Tarrant carried the crates out two at a time. The cold came in each time the door swung.

Between loads he stopped to talk to her. He asked if she meant to stay at the bakery. She said she did not know. He said that was the right answer at her age. Then he told her that he had driven the van for nineteen years and still did not know. She laughed at that. Voss looked up from the bench but did not tell them to be quiet.

The white loaves came out last. They were pale gold and split along the top where Voss had scored them. He lifted one and held it near his ear. Nell had seen him do this a hundred times. She had never asked what he heard. The crust made a small crackling sound as it cooled. That was the answer, perhaps.

At four Tarrant loaded the final crates and pulled the van door shut, and the sound of it went off the walls of the lane and down toward the water where the lamp on the pier had at last gone out. He came back in to say goodnight, though it was nearly morning, and he took a roll from the rack without asking because he had never once asked in nineteen years. Voss let him. The roll was still too hot to eat. Tarrant ate it upon the step with his hat pushed back and the steam going up past his face. Then he drove off toward the ferry.

The bakery was quiet after the van had gone. Nell scraped the bench with the plastic card. Voss washed the bowls in the deep sink. Neither of them spoke for some time. The ovens ticked as the heat went out of them. Grey light came up over the harbour. Gulls began on the roof.

Voss dried his hands and set a white loaf in front of her. He said it was hers to take home. She said it was a good one. He said it was an ordinary one, and that ordinary was the hardest kind to make. She wrapped it in a cloth and put it under her arm. The street outside smelled of salt and bread. She walked home the long way, by the water.

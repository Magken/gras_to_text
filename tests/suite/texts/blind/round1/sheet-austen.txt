Write in this shape. The subject is yours; take only the shape from the source. The examples are from the source: copy how they are built, not their people, places, or things, and do not reuse any run of four or more of their words. Start at the largest unit and go down.

Paragraph. 68 paragraphs. A paragraph holds about 1.9 sentences. About 2 in 10 paragraphs end on a short sentence. Such as: "What a fine thing for our girls!"
Example: It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife.

Sentence. Mix the lengths: about a quarter of sentences are 7 words or fewer, half fall between 7 and 21, and a quarter run 21 or more. A typical sentence is about 13 words. About 3 in 10 long sentences are followed at once by a short one, of 7 words or fewer; that is where much of the contrast sits. How sentences are built: about 5 in 10 are one clause. About 4 in 10 have two clauses: about 1 in 10 joined with and, but, or so, and about 3 in 10 with a clause hung on because, as, if, that, or who. About 1 in 21 have no main verb. Keep that mix. Do not cut a joined sentence into separate short ones. Commas: about 1.2 per sentence. There are semicolons in about 2 in 10 sentences, colons in a few sentences, dashes in a few sentences. Quoted speech appears in about 6 in 10 sentences. About 1 word in 7 is a pronoun (13.5%). Use a pronoun when the person was just named, and name them again when the paragraph moves on. The passive is rare (0.5%). Say who does the act.
Vary the sentence shapes. These are the most common, each with a sentence from the source:
- Open on someone already named, and go straight to what they did. Keep it to one clause. About 1 in 10 sentences. Example: "I desire you will do no such thing." It sits in the paragraphs about nerves, acquaintance, act.
- Open by tying this sentence back to the last one. Then hang a clause on because, as, if, that, or who. About 1 in 14 sentences. It often ends a paragraph. Example: "But it is very likely that he may fall in love with one of them, and therefore you must visit him as soon as he comes." It sits in the paragraphs about acquaintance, daughters, design.
- Open on someone already named, and go straight to what they did. Keep it to one clause. About 1 in 14 sentences. Example: "I see no occasion for that." It sits in the paragraphs about children, compassion, consideration.
- Open on the thing and where it sits before the verb. Then hang a clause on because, as, if, that, or who. About 1 in 18 sentences. It often opens a paragraph. Example: "The rest of the evening was spent in conjecturing how soon he would return Mr. Bennet’s visit, and determining when they should ask him to dinner." It sits in the paragraphs about answer, astonishment, dinner.
Sentences open with a pronoun in about 4 in 10, a noun in about 1 in 10, a conjunction in about 1 in 10. Keep to those openings.
Example: “You want to tell me, and I have no objection to hearing it.”

Word. Glue words, as the source uses them: the about 3 in 100 words, of about 3, to about 3, and about 2, a about 2, that about 1, in about 1. by, from and upon are rare, under 1 in 100 words; use them only now and then. These glue words carry the voice. Keep them at about those rates; a reply that drops them sounds clipped. shall and upon are older, formal words; the source uses them, so keep them, about as often as the source does. therefore and consequently are formal linking words; the source uses them, so keep them, about as often as the source does. About 1 word in 13 has three syllables or more, such as impossible, neighbourhood, acquaintance, invitation. Keep that level: do not swap them for short plain words, and do not add more.
Example: “Why, my dear, you must know, Mrs. Long says that Netherfield is taken by a young man of large fortune from the north of England; that he came down on Monday in a chaise and four to see the place, and was so much delighted with it that he agreed with Mr. Morris immediately; that he is to take possession before Michaelmas, and some of his servants are to be in the house by the end of next week.”

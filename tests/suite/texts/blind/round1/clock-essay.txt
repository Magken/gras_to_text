The clock in the tower of St. Aldric had stopped at twenty past four on the Monday, and nobody in the village could say which twenty past four it had been. Miss Varley came up from the town on the Tuesday with her nephew Tobin and a canvas bag of tools that clinked against the ladder rungs. The vicar met them at the foot of the tower stair and said the wedding was on Saturday at eleven. He said it as if the hour itself were a guest who might not come. Miss Varley looked up the stair and did not answer. The stair was narrow and it turned twice before it reached the bell loft, so the light from the slit windows came in thin grey bars across the treads.

The movement sat in a wooden case at the top. It was older than the church roof. Tobin set the lamp on a beam and held it where she pointed. She opened the case and breathed in the smell of old oil. The wheels were brown with dust. One pin had worn thin upon its pivot. "There," she said.

Tobin was fifteen and had come because his mother had sent him. He wanted to be somewhere else that week. The girl who was to be married was a cousin of a friend of his, and he had seen her once at the summer fair. He did not say this to his aunt. His aunt knew it anyway. She knew most things that were said in a low voice. She handed him a brush and told him to begin at the top wheel.

They worked through the Wednesday with the tower door propped open by a stone, and the sound of the village came up the stair in pieces. A cart went by. Somewhere a woman was beating a rug against a wall, and each blow reached them a moment after Tobin saw the dust rise from it through the window. Miss Varley laid the parts out on a sheet of brown paper in the order that she had taken them from the case. Each part had a number written beside it in pencil. The pencil marks were small and certain, because she had learned long ago that memory was the first thing a clock would steal from you. Tobin asked her once whether she had ever lost a piece.

She told him about a clock in a manor house by the sea, where the damp had got into the case and a spring had broken in her hand as she lifted it out. She had been younger than his mother was now. The owner of the house had stood in the doorway and watched her gather the pieces from the floor, and he had not said a single word to her for the whole of that afternoon. "That was worse than shouting," she said. Tobin asked what she had done. She said she had made a new spring from the old one, filed and tempered at the kitchen fire of a cottage down the lane, because there was no other spring within forty miles. It kept time for thirty years. Tobin did not know if he believed her.

On the Thursday the bride came to the church. Her name was Hester Lane. She stood in the porch with her mother and looked at the flowers someone had left in buckets. Tobin saw her from the slit window. He put the brush down. Miss Varley did not look up from the escapement. "The clock will not strike any sooner if you watch her," she said.

The escapement was the difficult part. It was the heart of the thing and it had to let go and catch again at every tick. Miss Varley held the pallet up to the lamp. She turned it slowly between her finger and thumb. The faces were scored. She said a clock could run a long time on a wound like that. She said it could not run forever.

That evening they ate bread and cheese on the tower steps. The light went red over the fields beyond the churchyard wall. Tobin asked her why she had never married. She laughed at him. It was a short laugh and not an unkind one. She said she had been asked twice, and that both of the men had wanted her to stop. "Stop what?" Tobin said. She held up her hands.

The hands were brown and scarred at the knuckles. There was a white line across one thumb where a file had slipped. Tobin had seen those hands all his life. He had never thought of them as anything but his aunt's. Now he looked at them as he might look at a tool on a bench. They were very good tools. He did not say so.

On the Friday they put the movement back together. Tobin read the numbers from the paper and she fitted each part as he called it. The work went quickly. At noon she set the pendulum swinging with one finger. The clock ticked. It ticked again. Tobin found that he had been holding his breath.

They climbed to the dial that afternoon to set the hands, and the wind came in through the louvres of the bell loft and moved the dust along the boards in little drifts. Miss Varley leaned out through the small door behind the dial with Tobin holding the back of her coat in both fists. She said that the hands were the part of a clock that everyone saw and the part that mattered least, which was the reason that people argued about them the most. Below them the vicar was crossing the churchyard with a ladder over his shoulder. He waved. Miss Varley moved the minute hand round by the width of a finger and called for the time. Tobin read it to her from his father's watch, which had been wrong by two minutes for as long as anyone could remember. She took the two minutes off without being told.

The wedding was at eleven on the Saturday. Tobin and his aunt sat at the back of the church in their good coats. The clock struck the hour above them as Hester Lane came up the aisle. Every head turned upward at the first stroke. Then every head turned back to the bride. Tobin counted the strokes to the end. There were eleven.

Afterwards Miss Varley went up the stair alone to listen to the movement. Tobin waited for her in the porch among the buckets of flowers. The guests went past him laughing and did not see him. He did not mind that as much as he had thought he would. When his aunt came down she had dust on her sleeve and oil on one thumb. She said the pallet would last another ten years. He believed her.

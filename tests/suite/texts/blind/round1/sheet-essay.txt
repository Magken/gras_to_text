Write in this shape. The subject is yours; take only the shape from the source. The examples are from the source: copy how they are built, not their people, places, or things, and do not reuse any run of four or more of their words. Start at the largest unit and go down.

Paragraph. 13 paragraphs. A paragraph holds about 6.6 sentences. The long sentences (19 words or more) gather in paragraphs 1, 4, 5 and 11; the other paragraphs have one or none. About 4 in 10 paragraphs end on a short sentence. Such as: "You could keep it if you did not announce it."
Example: The kiln at the north end of the yard kept its heat through the night, and the bricks ticked as they cooled. Dr. Hale met Mr. Pell and Mrs. Adel before the noon bell, and they stood with their hands behind their backs as if the kiln might still be argued with. She had written the firing times in a book that smelled of ash. The first column was the hour. The second was the colour of the flame. The third was left blank upon purpose, because a blank line was the only honest mark for a thing she had not seen.

Sentence. Mix the lengths: about a quarter of sentences are 6 words or fewer, half fall between 6 and 19, and a quarter run 19 or more. A typical sentence is about 10 words. About 3 in 10 long sentences are followed at once by a short one, of 6 words or fewer; that is where much of the contrast sits. How sentences are built: about 6 in 10 are one clause. About 4 in 10 have two clauses: about 1 in 10 joined with and, but, or so, and about 3 in 10 with a clause hung on because, as, if, that, or who. A few have no main verb. Keep that mix. Do not cut a joined sentence into separate short ones. Commas: about 0.5 per sentence. There are no semicolons or colons or dashes. Do not add them. About 1 word in 12 is a pronoun (8.5%). Use a pronoun when the person was just named, and name them again when the paragraph moves on. The passive is rare (0.9%). Say who does the act.
Vary the sentence shapes. These are the most common, each with a sentence from the source:
- Open by naming the thing, then put was, had, or did before what it did. Keep it to one clause. About 2 in 10 sentences. Example: "The columns were for the room."
- Open by naming the thing, then put was, had, or did before what it did. Then join a second clause with and, but, or so. About 1 in 15 sentences. Example: "The argument was an old one and it had worn a groove, the way a latch wears a groove, and both of them knew which step came next." It sits in the paragraphs about bench, blank, book.
- Open on someone already named, and go straight to what they did. Keep it to one clause. About 1 in 15 sentences. Example: "She walked the length of the drying racks and touched nothing." It sits in the paragraphs about bench, chip, column.
- Open by naming the thing, then say what it did. Keep it to one clause. About 1 in 20 sentences. Example: "A boy carried shelves from the shed to the wall and set them down too hard." It sits in the paragraphs about awning, bell, bench.
Sentences open with a determiner in about 4 in 10, a pronoun in about 3 in 10, a noun in about 1 in 10. Keep to those openings.
Example: The pots on the upper rack were pale and dusty.

Word. Glue words, as the source uses them: the about 12 in 100 words, and about 5, a about 4, of about 2, that about 2, to about 1, in about 1. by, from and upon are rare, under 1 in 100 words; use them only now and then. These glue words carry the voice. Keep them at about those rates; a reply that drops them sounds clipped. upon is an older, formal word; the source uses it, so keep it, about as often as the source does.
Example: The book was in the inside pocket of her coat, and the third column was still blank, and the blank was still upon purpose.

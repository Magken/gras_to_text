The clock of St. Aldric's had stopped at twenty past four on the Monday. The whole village took it as a personal injury, because the Fenwick wedding was to be on Saturday.

"It cannot be left so," said Mrs. Tolley at the gate. "Imagine the bride coming up the path under a dead clock!"

Agnes Pell came up from the town on Tuesday with her tools in a carpet bag, and her nephew Thomas carried the rest. She was fifty and had mended clocks for thirty years; he was seventeen and had mended nothing at all.

"You will hold the lamp," she told him on the tower stair. "That is the first lesson, and it is a long one."

He held it. The stair was narrow, and the dust lay upon every step like flour.

"Why must we climb so far for one clock?"

"Because the clock lives up there, and it will not come down to us."

The chamber at the top was smaller than he had imagined. The movement stood in an iron frame in the middle of the floor, and rods ran off from it through the walls to the four faces. It smelled of old oil and pigeons.

Agnes walked twice around it before she touched anything. Then she knelt.

"There," she said. "Do you see the escape wheel?"

"I see a great many wheels."

"The small one with teeth like a saw. One tooth is bent, and consequently the pallet cannot catch it; the train runs a little and then locks."

Thomas bent closer with the lamp. He did not see it at first, and then he did, and it annoyed him that it had been so plain.

"Can you straighten it?"

"I can try, but I shall not promise. Brass remembers where it has been bent."

The vicar came up on Wednesday, breathing hard, and stood in the doorway with his hat in his hands. He was a mild, anxious man who believed that every misfortune in the parish was a comment upon his sermons.

"Miss Pell, I am told you have found the fault. Will it go by Saturday?"

"It will go by Thursday, if the wheel holds. Whether it will keep time is another question."

"But surely a clock that goes must keep time?"

"No, sir. A clock may go very cheerfully and be wrong all day."

He looked so unhappy at this that Thomas nearly laughed. Agnes gave him a look, and he did not.

They worked through the afternoon. She filed the tooth by the width of a hair, tried it, and filed again; Thomas held the lamp, then the pliers, then the lamp again.

"You are impatient," she said, without looking up.

"I am not."

"You have sighed eleven times. I have been counting, because it is more interesting than the wheel."

He went red. After a while he asked, very politely, whether she had ever failed with a clock.

"Often. At Hallam Cross I took the whole movement apart, and when I had done I had three pieces left over and no notion where they belonged."

"What did you do?"

"I put them in my pocket and told the churchwarden it was running better than ever. As it happens, it was."

On Thursday morning the wheel went back into the frame. Agnes set the pendulum swinging with one finger, and the two of them stood without speaking while the escapement took up its beat.

It ticked. It ticked again and went on ticking, heavy and slow, like somebody walking in a room below.

"There," said Thomas. "It goes."

"It goes. Now we shall see if it is honest."

They left it for the day and came back at evening with her pocket watch, which she had set by the railway clock in town. The church was four minutes fast.

"Four minutes!" said Thomas. "After all that!"

"Four minutes is nothing. The fault is in the bob upon the pendulum; a quarter turn will slow it, and therefore you shall make the turn."

His hands were not steady. She did not remark upon it, and he noticed that she did not and was grateful.

By Friday the village had heard the bell strike the hours again. People came to the gate to tell each other so, and Mrs. Tolley declared that she had never doubted it for a moment.

The Fenwick girl came herself in the afternoon, with her mother behind her. She was small and flushed and very determined to be pleased by everything.

"You have saved my wedding," she said to Agnes at the foot of the stair.

"I have mended your clock. Your wedding was never in any danger from it."

"Oh, but it would have been dreadful to be married at twenty past four for ever."

Agnes considered this seriously, as though it were a mechanical problem. Then she smiled.

"Yes," she said. "I suppose it would."

On Saturday they did not go up the tower. They stood among the guests at the back of the church in their best clothes, and at eleven the bell struck above them, exactly on the hour.

Thomas checked the pocket watch, which his aunt had given him to hold. It agreed.

"Well?" she said quietly.

"It is right. It is exactly right."

"Then remember that you turned the bob. I only told you which way."

He wanted to say something in answer, but the organ began and everyone rose to see the bride. So he said nothing; he only stood beside her and listened for the clock above the music, keeping its time.

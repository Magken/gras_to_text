The water had come down the valley in the spring and had not gone back, so the old road ran into it and stopped. Mr. Voss arrived on the first morning with a chain, a level, and a case of pencils that he had sharpened on the train, because he believed a surveyor should begin the day with nothing left to do but look. The guide was a woman named Ansel Brae who had been hired by the county for four shillings a day. She met him at the edge of the flood with a flat boat and a pole. He asked her how deep it was. She said it depended upon where he meant to stand, and that the valley had never agreed with itself about that even when it was dry. He wrote the answer down.

The boat was narrow and it leaned when he stepped in. Brae stood at the back and pushed off from a fence post. The post was still upright. Its wire ran out under the water toward nothing. Voss set the level across his knees and waited for the surface to settle. It did not settle. He marked the first station as a guess.

They crossed what had been a pasture. The grass showed below them like hair in a basin. Brae named each field as they passed it, and she named the families that had owned them. Voss asked her to stop. He said the map was for the ground and not for the people on it. She kept naming them, but more quietly. He found that he wrote the names in the margin anyway.

By the afternoon they had reached the church, which stood up to its windows in brown water and threw a long reflection that shivered whenever the wind moved. Voss wanted the tower for a fixed point, because it was the only thing in the valley that had not shifted since the old maps were drawn, and a surveyor without a fixed point is only a man in a boat. Brae tied the boat to a gutter. He climbed through a window with the level on his back and went up the stairs in the dark. The bell was still there. From the top he could see the whole flood laid out like a sheet of tin, and he took his bearings from three chimneys that he trusted less than he would have liked. When he came down she had lit a pipe.

That night they slept in a hayloft on the east ridge, where the farmer had moved his cows and his wife and whatever furniture the cart could hold. Voss worked by a lamp until late, drawing the day's line in pencil so that he could rub it out if the second day disagreed with the first. Brae watched him from the ladder. She told him that her father had walked this valley with a stick and a length of string, and that his marks were still cut into the gateposts if anyone cared to look for them under the water. Voss said he did not have the time to look under water. She said nobody did. The farmer's wife brought them bread.

The second day was grey. A mist lay on the flood and hid the far side. Brae poled them out along a line of willows that marked the old stream. The stream was under everything now. Voss could feel it pulling at the boat, as if it still remembered which way it ought to go. He took his readings slowly. Each one was a little different from the last.

At noon they found a drowned mill. The wheel had jammed with branches and a dead sheep hung in the spokes. Brae turned her face away from it. Voss measured the height of the waterline on the wall. It was higher than the mark from the week before. He wrote the number down and said nothing about it. She saw him write it.

"It is still rising," she said. He said that it might be. He said that the map would show the valley as it was on the day he drew it, and that a map could promise no more. Brae asked him who the map was for. The county, he said. She asked what the county meant to do with it. He did not know. He had never been asked.

The mist lifted in the late afternoon. The far ridge came out of it with a row of cottages along its crest. Smoke rose from two of them. The others were empty, and their doors stood open to the weather. Brae said the families had gone to the town. Voss asked whether they would come back. She said that depended upon the water.

On the third morning he could not find the fence post. Brae said it had gone over in the night. They started from the church instead. The tower gave him the same bearings as before, and he was glad of it. The chimneys had not moved. The water had. He measured again at the mill and did not need to look twice.

By the evening the map was nearly finished, and Voss laid it out on a board across the gunwales so that Brae could see what three days of work looked like when it was pressed flat and made to hold still. She studied it for a long time without speaking, running a finger along the line of the old stream, which he had drawn in blue although no one could see it any longer. He had kept her names in the margin after all, the whole column of them, in a hand smaller than the rest. She found her father's name near the bottom. She did not say anything about it. He had put it there on the first night, because he had not known what else a surveyor ought to do with a thing he was told but could not measure. The light went down behind the ridge.

They came back to the old road at dusk. The road still ran into the water and stopped. Brae held the boat steady while he climbed out with the case and the board. He paid her the twelve shillings. She counted them and put them in her coat. He asked whether she would guide the next man if the county sent one. She said she supposed so.

The map went to the county on the Friday. A clerk rolled it and put it in a drawer with the others. The valley on the paper was three days old and already wrong. Voss knew this when he signed it. He signed it anyway, because the date was written beside his name and the date was true. The names in the margin were true as well. Nobody had asked him for those.

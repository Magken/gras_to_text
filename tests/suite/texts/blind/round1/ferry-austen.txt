It is a custom long kept on the lower river, that no ferry shall cross after the lamps are lit, whatever the weather or the fare.

The Hale sisters kept it better than anyone. Agnes, who was the elder, held that a rule was only as good as the person who refused to break it; Ruth, who was nineteen, held no opinion on rules at all.

"There is a man on the far landing," said Ruth, at the window. "He has been waving this quarter of an hour."

"Then he has been wasting his arm."

"He has a lantern, and a bag, and I think a very good coat."

Agnes did not look up from the ledger. She had been adding the week's fares, and they came, as they always did in January, to less than the week's coal.

"A good coat does not make the ice any thinner," she said.

"But it may make the fare thicker." Ruth pressed her face to the glass. "He is waving again. He looks cold."

"Everyone looks cold in January. That is the particular character of the month."

The house stood on the bank above the ferry stage, and from its upper window one could see the whole breadth of the river, black between its white margins. The ice had come in from the edges a week before and had stopped, as if it were considering the matter.

Ruth came away from the window at last, and sat down by the fire with an air of great injury.

"You are very hard, Agnes."

"I am very sensible. It is not the same thing, though I grant it is often mistaken for it."

"Our father would have gone."

"Our father did go, once, after dark." Agnes closed the ledger. "You were too young to remember the consequence, and I was not."

That silenced Ruth for a time. The clock on the mantel struck seven, and the fire settled, and the lantern on the far side went on swinging in its small patient arc.

It was Agnes who went to the window next. She told herself that she went only to draw the curtain.

"He has sat down upon the bench," she said.

"Has he?" Ruth was at her shoulder in an instant. "Then he means to stay the night there. He will be frozen by morning, and it will be entirely your doing."

"It will be entirely his, for coming so late to a river he does not know."

"How can you tell that he does not know it?"

"Because anyone who knew it would not be sitting there."

Ruth considered this, and found it unanswerable, which only made her crosser. She took up her shawl, and put it down, and took it up again.

"I shall go alone, then," she said.

"You shall do no such thing. You cannot manage the pole in open water, and you know it."

"Then come with me, and manage it yourself."

Agnes looked at her sister for a long moment. There was, in Ruth's face, that particular brightness which in their mother had preceded every imprudence of her life; and Agnes, who had loved their mother, had therefore learned to dread it.

"If I go," she said slowly, "it is because I would rather drown with you than explain to the parish why I let you drown alone."

"That is the kindest thing you have ever said to me."

"It was not meant kindly."

They went down together. The cold at the stage was of that dry, ringing sort which seems to make every sound carry twice as far; the ferry creaked against its rope as they stepped aboard, and across the water the lantern stopped swinging.

The crossing took twenty minutes, which was twice its summer length. Agnes worked the pole with a steady, unforgiving stroke, and Ruth held the lamp, and neither of them spoke.

The stranger stood as they drew near. He was younger than Agnes had supposed, and thinner, and the good coat was a good deal older than it had looked from the window.

"You are very kind," he said. "I had quite given you up."

"We had quite given you up too," said Agnes. "My sister was of another mind."

He looked at Ruth, and bowed, and she coloured in the lamplight in a manner which Agnes observed and did not like.

"What is your business on our side, sir, at such an hour?"

"I have a letter to deliver at the mill house. It is from a man who owes the miller a considerable sum, and who is dying, and who wished him to know that it would be paid."

"That is a strange errand for a night like this."

"It is a strange man who sent me. He was particular that it should be tonight."

Agnes said nothing to that. She held the boat steady while he stepped down, and then she turned it into the current.

Halfway across, the ice at the eastern margin gave a long, low groan, like a door opening somewhere in a large house. Ruth caught her breath. The stranger did not move at all.

"It does that," said Agnes. "It means nothing."

"Does it?" said he. "I had heard that it meant a great deal."

"Then you have heard more than I have, and I have lived here all my life."

They reached the stage without further incident. The stranger paid double the fare, in silver, and thanked them both, and went up the lane towards the mill with his lantern swinging before him.

Ruth watched until the light was gone.

"There," she said. "Now confess that I was right."

Agnes was looking at the coins in her palm. They were very old, and very worn, and one of them bore the head of a king who had been dead for eighty years.

"I confess nothing," she said, "until I have spoken to the miller in the morning."

But in the morning there was no letter at the mill house. The miller had never heard of any debt, nor of any dying man; and the only footprints in the lane were their own.

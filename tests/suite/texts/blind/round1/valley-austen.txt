Mr. Hale arrived at the valley on the first morning of the flood; he had been sent from the capital to measure what the water had taken, and he did not know the ground at all.

"You will want a guide," said the innkeeper. "Orrin knows every field that is under the lake now."

Orrin came to the door with his boots already wet. He looked at the surveyor's instruments with a patience that was not quite respect.

"I suppose you shall want the old boundaries," he said. "They are all beneath the water, sir, but I can tell you where they lie."

"That is precisely what I require," said Mr. Hale. "The commission must know how much land is lost, because the tenants will ask for their rents to be forgiven."

"Then they shall ask a great deal."

They set out in a flat boat before noon. The valley lay under a sheet of brown water, and the tops of hedges rose from it like the backs of drowned animals.

Mr. Hale fixed his theodolite upon the stern and took his first bearing. Orrin held the boat steady with a pole, and he named each place as they passed it.

"That is Pellow's orchard. That is the lane to the mill, if you can call it a lane now. And there, where the elm is standing, the church meadow ended."

"How can you be certain of the elm?" asked Mr. Hale.

"Because my father cut his name in it, and I cut mine below his."

The surveyor wrote this down without comment. He had learned long ago that a local memory was worth more than a parish map, though he never said so to the commission.

By evening they had fixed eleven points. Mr. Hale drew them upon his sheet by lamplight at the inn, and Orrin watched him from the settle by the fire.

"It looks very small on paper," said Orrin.

"Everything does. That is the use of paper."

On the second day the rain returned. It came across the hills in long grey curtains, and consequently the water rose another hand's breadth before they reached the mill.

"We shall lose the lower marks," said Mr. Hale. "The stones I set yesterday will be covered by tonight."

"Then you must trust me instead of the stones," said Orrin. "I have no objection to being trusted."

Mr. Hale laughed, which surprised them both. He had not laughed since he left the city.

They worked through the afternoon in silence, except for the numbers. Orrin called the distances as he paced them along the ridge, and the surveyor repeated each one before he wrote it.

"Forty chains to the ford. Twelve to the sheepfold. The rest is water."

"You say that as if it were a small matter."

"It is not small to me," said Orrin. "My sister's house was by the ford."

Mr. Hale put down his pencil. He asked whether the sister was safe, and Orrin told him she had gone to her husband's people in the next county, where the ground was high and the neighbours were unpleasant.

"Therefore she is safe and miserable," said Mr. Hale.

"She is both. She writes that she prefers the flood."

That night the surveyor could not sleep. He sat at the window and considered the sheet, which now showed a valley that no longer existed, drawn with the confidence of a man who had never seen it dry.

He thought that perhaps the commission did not want a map at all. It wanted a number, and a number could be argued with more easily than a memory.

On the third morning the sky cleared. The water had begun, very slowly, to fall.

"You see," said Orrin, pointing from the boat, "the hedge by Pellow's has come up again. The orchard will be there by Sunday, or what is left of it."

"Then my figures are already wrong."

"Your figures were never right, sir. They were only yours."

Mr. Hale considered this for some time; he did not find it an insult. He asked Orrin to take him once more along the line of the old lane, so that he might record where the ground was rising.

They followed the elm, the mill, the ford. At each place Orrin said what had been there, and the surveyor wrote what was there now, and between the two columns lay the whole of the damage.

"What shall you tell them in the capital?" asked Orrin.

"I shall tell them that the valley is larger than it looks," said Mr. Hale, "because a man who lived in it can still walk every field under the water."

"They will not understand that."

"No. But it is true, and I am obliged to report what is true."

In the afternoon they brought the boat back to the inn. The innkeeper asked whether the survey was finished, and Mr. Hale said it was as finished as anything could be when the ground itself would not keep still.

He paid Orrin more than the commission allowed. Orrin looked at the coins and then at the surveyor.

"This is too much."

"It is exactly the value of the elm," said Mr. Hale, "and of your father's name upon it."

Orrin put the money in his coat without another word. He walked down to the water's edge, where the first line of mud was drying in the sun, and he stood there a long while, looking at what had come back.

Marit had kept the ferry for eleven winters, and her sister Anne had kept Marit, which she considered by far the harder office of the two.

"You will not take the boat out again tonight," said Anne, when the lamp had been lit and the kettle set upon the stove. "I have decided it for you, since you never decide anything sensible for yourself."

"I have no intention of taking it anywhere."

"You say that every evening, and every evening somebody knocks."

Marit laughed at this, because it was true, and because she could think of no better answer. The river beyond their window was black and slow, with ice gathering along its edges like lace upon a sleeve.

It was nearly nine o'clock when the knock came. Anne looked at her sister with an expression of perfect triumph; Marit looked at the door.

"Shall I send him away?" Anne asked. "I should enjoy it very much."

"You do not know that it is a him."

"I know it by the knock. Women knock as if they were apologising, and men knock as if they had paid for the house."

The stranger was indeed a man, tall and rather thin, with snow on his shoulders and a leather case held against his chest. He removed his hat at once, which Anne privately allowed to be a point in his favour.

"I beg your pardon," he said. "I was told in the village that you carry passengers across, and I must be on the other side before morning, if it can possibly be managed."

"Who told you that?" said Anne. "Whoever it was ought to be ashamed of himself."

"A woman at the inn. She said you were the only people on this stretch of river who would not ask me my business."

Marit smiled. "She was right about me, though I cannot promise it for my sister."

"You are being teased, sir, and you are also being refused," said Anne. "The ice is coming in from the bank, the current is stronger than it looks, and I have no wish to be a widow's sister before I am forty."

"You are not married," said Marit.

"That is precisely my point; I should be nobody's widow at all, only a sister without a sister, which is worse."

The stranger set his case upon the table, carefully, as if it held something that might break. He did not sit down, although Marit offered him the chair.

"I would pay whatever you think fair," he said. "I would pay double. I have a letter that must be delivered, and if it is not delivered by tomorrow it will be of no use to anybody."

"Then it is of very little use tonight," said Anne, "and I do not see why my sister should drown for it."

Marit had already taken down her coat. She did it slowly, so that Anne might have time to argue, and Anne took the time and used every minute of it.

"You are impossible," she said at last. "Mother said so when you were six, and consequently I have had thirty years in which to grow used to it, and I have not."

"You shall come with us, then, and keep me from drowning."

"I shall do nothing of the kind."

She put on her own coat nevertheless, and her boots, and a woollen scarf wound twice about her head. The stranger watched these preparations with an astonishment he made no effort to conceal.

"You are both coming?"

"We are always both coming," said Marit. "She rows and I steer, and between us we have lost nobody yet."

Outside, the cold was very still. Their breath rose white in the lantern-light, and the boat lay against the landing like a sleeping animal that did not wish to be disturbed.

Anne took the oars without a word. Marit settled the stranger in the bow, where he held his case upon his knees and his hat upon his case, and she pushed them off from the stones.

"May I ask what is in the letter?" said Marit, when they were halfway across. "You need not answer me, of course."

"It is a proposal," he said. "Of marriage. I wrote it three times and burnt it twice, and her father leaves for the city in the morning and takes her with him."

Anne stopped rowing. The boat turned a little in the current before she remembered herself.

"You brought us out on a night like this for a proposal?"

"I did, and I am sorry for it. But I thought that if I did not go tonight, I should never find the courage again, and I have been a coward about her for four years."

"Four years!" cried Anne. "She will have given you up long ago, I should think."

"Perhaps she has. That is what the letter is meant to discover."

Marit watched her sister's face in the lantern-light, and she saw it soften in a manner that it seldom did for anybody.

They reached the far landing a little before ten. The stranger climbed out, stood a moment on the frozen boards, and then turned back and pressed more coins into Marit's hand than she could count in the dark.

"Thank you," he said. "Both of you. I shall not forget it."

"See that she does not either," said Anne, "or I shall row across and tell her myself what trouble you gave us."

He laughed, and bowed, and went up the bank towards the light. They watched him until he was gone.

Anne rowed home more slowly than she had rowed out, and she did not complain once about the ice. Marit thought this very remarkable.

"You liked him," she said.

"I liked his letter. I do not know him well enough to like him, and I never shall, since he will be married by spring and too happy to cross rivers for anybody."

"You might have been married yourself, if you had ever let anybody knock twice."

Anne pulled hard upon the oars, so that the spray flew up and caught her sister full in the face, and she did not apologise for it at all.

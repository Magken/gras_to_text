The water had come up in March and not gone down, and by the time Aurel Venn arrived with his theodolite in its oiled case, the people of the upper farms had stopped calling it a flood. They called it the lake. They said it the way you might name a stranger who has stayed long enough to be given a chair.

The guide they sent him was a woman named Ilse Marr, who had grown up at the bottom of the valley in a house that was now eleven feet under. She met him at the ridge road with a flat-bottomed boat roped to a handcart and did not offer to shake hands. Her hands were busy with the rope. He took that as a kind of courtesy.

On the first day they worked the eastern shore. Venn set up the tripod on whatever held still: a stone wall's shoulder, the roof ridge of a byre, and once the concrete lid of a well that stood a hand's breadth above the surface like a coin laid on a table. Ilse held the staff. She held it straighter than the boys he usually hired, and she did not fidget while he read the angles. When he called out a number she repeated it back, and after the third station he stopped checking her.

The water was very clear. That surprised him. He had expected mud, and there was mud at the edges, but further out the valley lay under glass. He could see fence lines running down into green dark, and a field of barley standing drowned and upright, every stalk still in its row. A cart sat in a lane with its shafts raised as if someone had just stepped away to open a gate.

"That's Hesker's," Ilse said, when she saw him looking. "He'll want it back."

"Will he get it?"

She shrugged, and the boat rocked with her. "He'll want it."

That night they slept in the loft of the schoolhouse, which stood high enough to have been spared, and Venn inked the day's readings by lamplight while Ilse lay on her back with her boots still on. He drew the shoreline as a firm line, the way the office would expect. Then he sat looking at it. It was the only line on the sheet he knew to be false. The water had been a finger higher at noon than at dawn. He did not know which one to draw.

On the second day the wind came from the south and roughed the surface, and the drowned valley disappeared under it. They rowed out to the church. Only the tower stood clear, with the bell still hanging in it, and Venn climbed the ladder inside, through pigeon muck and the smell of wet timber, to set up on the bell platform. From there he could see the whole of the lake: the long bend of it, the three farms on the western slope that it had not quite reached, and the road going in at the north end and not coming out.

Ilse stayed in the boat below, holding it off the stonework with an oar. When he shouted down for her to row out to the next mark she did not move straight away. She was looking into the water beside the tower, where the wind had dropped for a moment and the surface had cleared.

"The graves," she said, when he asked. She said nothing else about it, and he did not ask again. He took his readings, and she rowed where he pointed, and the numbers went down into the book in his small, even hand.

In the afternoon they found the house. He hadn't known it was hers until she shipped the oars and let the boat drift over it. The roof was gone, lifted off and carried somewhere, so they were looking straight down into the rooms. There was a table. There was a dresser with the plates still on the rack, because the water had come up slowly and set everything down gently, as a careful person would. A child's shoe lay on the stair.

"My brother's," Ilse said. "From when he was small. My mother kept it."

Venn thought of several things to say. He said none of them. After a while she took up the oars again, and he marked the spot on his sheet with a small cross, which in the office's system of symbols meant a building, ruined.

The third day was still and bright. They closed the traverse along the western slope, and the last reading came out within two seconds of arc of where it should have been. It was the best closure he had made in years. He told her so, and she nodded as if he had told her the time.

They beached the boat at the ridge road in the evening. While she coiled the rope he unfolded the sheet on the handcart and showed her the valley as he had drawn it: the contours running down under the blue wash, every field and wall and lane he could fix from the surface, the church, the crosses.

She looked at it for a long time.

"You've put the water in," she said.

"It's there."

"It is now." She laid one finger on the blue, just where the house would be. "You'll send this to the city, and in ten years someone will open a drawer and think it was always a lake."

He had no answer to that. He folded the sheet along its creases and put it in the case with the instrument. In the morning, before the cart came, he opened it again and, below the scale bar, in the smallest hand he had, wrote the date and the words *water level as observed*. It wasn't much. It was the only true thing he knew how to add.

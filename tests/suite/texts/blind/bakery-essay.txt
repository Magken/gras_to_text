The ovens at the back of the shop on Quay Street were lit at eleven, and the tiles above them sweated before the first tray went in. Ansel Brine had kept the bakery for thirty years and he still counted the loaves aloud as if the harbour might dispute the number. The apprentice was a girl called Tessa who had come to him in the spring with flour already in her hair. She weighed the dough on a brass scale that had belonged to his father. The flour sacks leaned against the wall. The window was dark. Out past the railings the boats knocked against the pier in a slow order that nobody on the street had ever tried to change.

Tessa did not talk while she worked. Ansel liked that. The dough was cold from the cellar and it fought her hands. She pressed it down and folded it and pressed it again. The clock over the door said a quarter past twelve. A gull walked the sill outside and looked in at the light. Ansel told her the gull came every night because the bins behind the shop were full of crusts.

The first batch was the plain white. The second was the rye, which took longer and smelled of the cellar. Ansel set the timer and then ignored it. He knew the bread by the sound of the crust. Tessa watched him open the oven door a hand's width and listen. She wrote the times in a notebook anyway. A written time was a thing she could check in the morning when the old baker was asleep.

At one o'clock the van came down the hill from the station road with its headlamps off, because the driver thought the lamps woke the houses and a woman in a dressing gown had told him so once. The driver was Colm Rees, and Colm had driven the harbour round for longer than Tessa had been alive. He came in by the side door and stood by the racks with his cap in his hand. The van ticked in the street behind him. Colm said the tide was high and the fish market would open early, so the café on the corner wanted two dozen more rolls than usual. Ansel did not look up from the trays. A late order is still an order.

Ansel said the rolls would be ready at four, and four was the time the rolls were always ready, and the café knew that as well as the tide did. Colm said the café had asked for half past three. The two men had said these same lines on other nights and the lines had worn smooth, like the step at the door that everybody used and nobody swept. Tessa went on shaping rolls. She made them small and even and set them in rows of six upon the tray. The rows were straight because Ansel had once made her start a whole tray again over a single crooked roll. Colm watched the rows for a while and then sat down on the flour sacks to wait.

The shop was warm now. Steam ran down the inside of the window. Colm took a heel of rye from the cooling rack and ate it without asking. Ansel saw and said nothing. The silence was a courtesy, or it was only that his hands were full. Tessa could not tell which, and she let the question go. She pushed the next tray toward the oven with her hip.

At two the power flickered. The lights over the bench went brown and came back. Ansel stood very still with the peel in his hands. The ovens were gas and would not care, but the mixer was electric and the mixer had the last of the dough in it. The lights held. Colm said the wind was getting up off the water. Ansel put the peel down and breathed out.

Tessa asked Colm about the round. He told her the order of the stops as if he were saying a prayer. The café came first, then the hotel with the blue door, then the school kitchen at the top of the hill. The last stop was an old man on the sea wall who took one small loaf and paid on Fridays. Colm said the old man had not paid for three weeks. He left the loaf anyway. Tessa wrote the old man into her notebook beside the times.

The rolls came out at twenty to four. They were pale on top and brown at the edges. Ansel tapped one and held it to his ear. Tessa had seen him do this a hundred times and still did not know what he heard. He nodded. Colm stood up from the sacks and brushed the flour off his trousers. The flour stayed where it was.

They loaded the van in the dark. The street was wet and the cobbles shone under the one lamp by the chandler's. Tessa carried the trays and Colm stacked them. Ansel stayed in the doorway with his arms folded. The harbour was loud with rigging. A rope slapped a mast somewhere and kept on slapping. Nobody went down to see which boat it was.

When the last tray was in, Colm shut the doors of the van and stood a moment with his hand flat on the cold metal, as though the van were a horse that needed to be told the road. Ansel told him the café would have its rolls at four and not a minute before. Colm said that the café would have them at ten to, because the hill ran downhill all the way. The old argument ended there, where it ended every night, with neither man the winner and both of them content with the draw. Tessa laughed. Colm climbed into the cab and let the van roll without the engine until it reached the corner. Then the engine caught and the sound went up the hill toward the station road.

Inside the shop the ovens were cooling. Ansel scraped the bench with the flat of a knife. Tessa swept the flour into a pile by the door. The pile was grey with grit from the street. She asked him if the old man on the sea wall would pay this Friday. Ansel said probably not. He said it the way a man reports the weather.

At half past four the sky over the water went from black to the colour of wet slate. The first gulls came down onto the quay. Ansel took off his apron and hung it on the nail. Tessa closed her notebook and put it in her coat. The times were all there, and the old man was there, and a clean line waited at the bottom for tomorrow. She had left that line empty upon purpose. The next night would fill it without any help from her.

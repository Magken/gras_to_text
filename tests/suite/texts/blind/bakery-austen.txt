The oven had been lit at two, and by half past it had begun to tick like a clock that could not agree with itself about the hour.

Mr. Aldous Penhale stood before it with his sleeves rolled and his hands folded upon his apron, as if the heat were a guest he was obliged to receive. He did not look round when the door opened.

"You are late, Tomas, and the rye will not wait for you."

"I am not late," said the apprentice, hanging his coat on the nail. "The church struck two as I turned the corner, and I ran the last of it because I knew you would say exactly that."

Penhale was glad of him, though he would sooner have burned the loaves than admit it. He pointed with his chin toward the long table, where the dough lay under cloths like sleepers who had been told to keep still.

"Knock it back, then. Gently; it has had a harder night than you."

Tomas set to work. The room smelled of flour and wet stone, and beyond the window the harbour lay black, with only the lamp on the breakwater to show that there was any water there at all.

For a while neither of them spoke. It was the hour that belonged to hands.

"May I ask you something?" said Tomas at last, turning a round of dough upon itself. "Why do you always put the first tray in on the left, when the right side of the oven is the hotter one?"

"Because the first tray is always the worst, and I would rather it came out pale than came out ruined." Penhale considered this, and added, "Also my father did it, and I have never found a reason good enough to quarrel with a dead man."

Tomas laughed; he had a laugh that went up at the end, as though it were asking permission. He did not stop working while he did it, and Penhale noticed that too.

The loaves went in at three. The old man shut the iron door with his hip, a movement so practised that it seemed less an act than a habit of the building itself.

"Now we shall see what kind of night it is."

"You say that every morning," said Tomas.

"And every morning it is a different kind, which is precisely why I say it."

At a quarter to four a van drew up outside, its engine coughing in the narrow street, and a woman climbed down from it with a clipboard under one arm and a cigarette she had not lit behind her ear. She knocked twice on the glass, though the door was plainly open.

"Morning, Mr. Penhale. Morning, Tomas. I have the Harbour Hotel, the two cafés on Quay Row, and that new place by the chandlery that wants its sourdough sliced, if you can believe it."

"I cannot," said Penhale. "Tell them I do not slice."

Ruth Amory was not surprised; she had delivered for him for six years and had never once expected him to change his mind. She leaned against the doorframe and let the warmth reach her.

"I told them you would say that. They said, and I quote, that they would pay extra for the inconvenience."

"Then they may pay extra for a knife."

Tomas looked from one to the other, uncertain whether he was meant to be amused. Ruth caught his eye and winked, and he decided that he was.

"You'll have to forgive him," she said. "He was born before sliced bread, and he has consequently never forgiven it."

"I was born before a great many things," said Penhale, "and I find that most of them improved nothing."

The first tray came out at twenty past four. It was pale on the left, as he had promised, and golden on the right, and the crust sang as it cooled, a thin crackling that Tomas had learned to listen for before he learned to see it.

"Hear that?" said Penhale. "That is the only applause a baker gets. You had better learn to like it."

"I like it already."

"Good. Then you may carry it out to the van, and Ruth will tell you how badly you have stacked the crates, and you will like that rather less."

Ruth took the crates as Tomas brought them, rearranging each one without comment, which was worse than if she had commented. When the van was full she stood a moment in the street, looking out at the water, where the first grey was beginning to separate the sky from the sea.

"It's going to be fine today," she said. "You can always tell by the gulls; if they're quiet at this hour, it means they're saving themselves for something."

"That is not true," said Penhale from the doorway.

"It is true if you want it to be," said Ruth, and she lit her cigarette at last, and climbed into the van, and was gone round the corner before either of them could answer her.

Tomas stood beside the old man in the doorway. Behind them the second tray was already browning, and the smell of it came out past their shoulders into the street, where a cat had appeared from nowhere to sit upon the step.

"Will she be back tomorrow?" he asked.

"She will be back every day until one of us dies, and I suspect it will not be her." Penhale turned to go inside, then stopped. "You did well tonight. Do not let it go to your head, because tomorrow the rye will remember that you were late."

"I wasn't late."

"No," said the old man, and something that was nearly a smile crossed his face before he could prevent it. "No, you were not."
